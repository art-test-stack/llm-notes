Learning objectives

You should be able to define a stochastic process and Brownian motion, interpret every term in an SDE, apply Itô’s lemma, connect the generator to the Fokker–Planck equation, simulate an SDE, and explain why a learned score lets a diffusion model run a noising process backward.

1. Stochastic-process foundations

A stochastic process is a family of random variables {X_t : t ∈ T} on one probability space. The index is often time. Fixing t gives a random variable X_{t}; fixing an outcome \omega gives a sample path t \mapsto X_{t}(\omega ). The state may be scalar or vector-valued, and time may be discrete or continuous.

Finite-dimensional distributions

For every finite collection t_{1},\ldots ,t_{n}, the joint law of (X_{t1},\ldots ,X_{\text{tn}}) is a finite-dimensional distribution. The consistent collection of all such laws specifies the process in distribution, although path properties such as continuity require choosing an appropriate version of the process.

Stationarity

  • Strict stationarity: shifting all times by the same amount leaves every finite-dimensional distribution unchanged.
  • Weak stationarity: the mean is constant and the covariance depends only on the lag: \operatorname{Cov}(X_{s},X_{t})=C(t-s).

Brownian motion itself is not stationary because \operatorname{Var}(W_{t})=t, but its increments are stationary: W_{t+h}-W_t depends in law only on h.

Markov property

A process is Markov if, conditional on the present, its future does not depend on its earlier history:

P(X_t ∈ A | X_s, history before s) = P(X_t ∈ A | X_s), s < t.

This does not mean the variables are independent. It means the current state contains all history needed for predicting the future under the model. If it does not, the state can sometimes be enlarged until it does.

Filtration and adaptedness

A filtration (ℱ_t) is an increasing family of information sets: ℱ_s ⊆ ℱ_t for s \le t. Think of ℱ_t as everything observable by time t. A process is adapted when X_{t} is known from ℱ_t; it cannot use future information. For an Itô integral, the integrand must satisfy a slightly stronger measurability condition, commonly ensured by progressive or predictable processes. At interview level, the key point is: the decision multiplying the next noise increment must be based only on present and past information.

2. Brownian motion

A standard one-dimensional Brownian motion (W_{t})_{t\ge 0} satisfies:

  1. W_{0}=0 almost surely;
  2. increments over disjoint intervals are independent;
  3. W_t-W_s ∼ Normal(0,t-s) for 0\le s\lt t;
  4. sample paths are continuous almost surely.

Thus variance grows linearly with elapsed time: \operatorname{Var}(W_{t}-W_{s})=t-s. A typical increment over a small interval \Delta t has magnitude \sqrt{\Delta t}, not \Delta t. This scaling is the source of the extra second-order term in Itô calculus.

Continuity, nondifferentiability, and quadratic variation

Brownian paths are continuous but almost surely nowhere differentiable. A difference quotient has scale (W_{t+h}-W_{t})/h \approx \sqrt{h}/h = 1/\sqrt{h}, which becomes more irregular as h→0.

For a partition 0=t_{0}\lt \ldots \lt t_{n}=t whose mesh tends to zero, Brownian motion has quadratic variation

Σᵢ (W_{tᵢ₊₁}-W_{tᵢ})² → t in probability.

Ordinary differentiable paths have zero quadratic variation. Informally this gives the Itô bookkeeping rules (d W_{t})^{2}=d t, d t\cdot d W_{t}=0, and (d t)^{2}=0.

3. Stochastic differential equations

An Itô SDE is written

dX_t = b(X_t,t) dt + σ(X_t,t) dW_t, X_0 ∼ μ₀.

This is shorthand for the integral equation

X_t = X_0 + ∫₀ᵗ b(X_s,s) ds + ∫₀ᵗ σ(X_s,s) dW_s.
  • The drift b is the local deterministic rate of motion.
  • The diffusion \sigma scales and correlates random fluctuations. In several dimensions, \sigma is a matrix and the instantaneous covariance is a=\sigma \sigma ^{\top}.
  • The initial condition may be fixed or random. For a strong solution it is independent of future Brownian increments and measurable at time zero.

For a small step h, conditional on X_{t}=x, the increment is approximately Gaussian:

X_{t+h}-X_t ≈ b(x,t)h + σ(x,t)√h Z, Z ∼ Normal(0,I).

Strong and weak solutions

  • A strong solution is constructed on a given probability space using a given Brownian motion; X is adapted to that noise. The question is whether the path is a measurable function of the supplied initial state and noise.
  • A weak solution allows the probability space and Brownian motion to be part of the construction. It asks only for a process with the required law and SDE dynamics.

These terms describe solution concepts, not numerical accuracy. Under standard global Lipschitz and linear-growth conditions on b and \sigma, a unique strong solution exists. Weaker coefficients require more care: existence, uniqueness in law, and pathwise uniqueness are distinct questions.

4. Itô calculus

Itô integral intuition

The Itô integral is the mean-square limit of left-endpoint sums

∫₀ᵗ H_s dW_s ≈ Σᵢ H_{tᵢ}(W_{tᵢ₊₁}-W_{tᵢ}).

Using the left endpoint makes H_{tᵢ} depend only on information available before the new Brownian increment. For square-integrable adapted H, the integral has mean zero and obeys the Itô isometry:

E[(∫₀ᵗ H_s dW_s)²] = E[∫₀ᵗ H_s² ds].

Itô’s lemma

If d X_{t}=b(X_{t},t)d t+\sigma (X_{t},t)d W_{t} and f(x,t) is sufficiently smooth, then in one dimension

df(X_t,t) = [∂_t f + b ∂_x f + ½σ² ∂_xx f](X_t,t) dt + [σ ∂_x f](X_t,t) dW_t.

The ordinary chain rule lacks the \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma ^{2}\partial _xx f term. It survives because the second-order Taylor term contains (d X_{t})^{2}\approx \sigma ^{2}(d W_{t})^{2}=\sigma ^{2}d t. Higher-order terms vanish at the relevant scale.

Worked example: geometric Brownian motion

Suppose d X_{t}=\mu X_{t} d t+\sigma X_{t} d W_{t} with X_{0}\gt 0. Apply Itô’s lemma to f(x)=\operatorname{log} x, for which f'(x)=1/x and f''(x)=-1/x^{2}:

d log X_t = (1/X_t)dX_t + ½(-1/X_t²)(σ²X_t²)dt = (μ-½σ²)dt + σdW_t.

Integrating and exponentiating gives

X_t = X_0 exp((μ-½σ²)t + σW_t).

The correction -\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma ^{2}t is exactly what the ordinary chain rule would miss. It also ensures E[X_{t}]=X_{0e}^{\mu t}.

5. Infinitesimal generator

For a time-homogeneous diffusion and a smooth test function f, the infinitesimal generator is

(ℒf)(x) = lim_{h↓0} [E(f(X_{t+h}) | X_t=x)-f(x)]/h = b(x)·∇f(x) + ½ Tr(a(x)∇²f(x)), a=σσᵀ.

Itô’s lemma shows why: the stochastic-integral term has conditional mean zero, so \mathcal{L}f gives the instantaneous expected evolution of the observable f(X_t). More generally, for a time-dependent observable, the drift is (\partial _t+\mathcal{L}_t)f.

Backward Kolmogorov equation

Let u(t,x)=E[\phi (X_{T}) \mid X_{t}=x]. Under regularity conditions,

∂_t u(t,x) + ℒ_t u(t,x) = 0, u(T,x)=φ(x).

It is called backward because the terminal payoff is specified at T and the equation determines its conditional value at earlier times. The generator acts on functions of the state; its adjoint acts on probability densities.

6. Fokker–Planck equation

Let p(x,t) be the density of X_{t}. From the generator identity,

d/dt E[f(X_t)] = E[(ℒ_t f)(X_t)] = ∫ (ℒ_t f)(x) p(x,t) dx.

But E[f(X_{t})]=\int f(x)p(x,t)d x. Moving derivatives from f onto p by integration by parts identifies the adjoint \mathcal{L}_t\cdot and yields the Fokker–Planck, or forward Kolmogorov, equation:

∂_t p = ℒ_t* p = -Σᵢ ∂_{xᵢ}(bᵢ p) + ½ Σᵢ,ⱼ ∂_{xᵢ}∂_{xⱼ}(aᵢⱼ p).

The drift transports probability; diffusion spreads it according to the local covariance a=\sigma \sigma ^{\top}. Boundary terms must vanish or be handled through explicit absorbing, reflecting, or flux conditions.

One-dimensional form and probability conservation

For d X_{t}=b(X_{t},t)d t+\sigma (X_{t},t)d W_{t},

∂_t p(x,t) = -∂_x[b(x,t)p(x,t)] + ½ ∂_xx[σ²(x,t)p(x,t)].

Define probability current J=\text{bp}-\tfrac{1}{2}\partial _x(\sigma ^{2}p). Then \partial _t p=-\partial _\text{xJ}, a continuity equation. Integrating over space gives

d/dt ∫ p(x,t)dx = -J(+∞,t)+J(-∞,t) = 0

when the boundary flux vanishes, so total probability is conserved. As a check, pure Brownian motion has b=0, \sigma =1, hence \partial _t p=\tfrac{1}{2}\partial _xx p, the heat equation whose density is Normal(0,t).

7. Numerical simulation

Euler–Maruyama

Euler–Maruyama freezes the coefficients over a step h and samples the Brownian increment as \Delta W_{n}=\sqrt{h} Z_{n}:

input: X0, terminal time T, number of steps N
h ← T / N
x ← X0
for n = 0, …, N-1:
    t ← n h
    z ← sample Normal(0, I)
    x ← x + b(x,t) h + σ(x,t) sqrt(h) z
return x

Store each x if a whole path is needed. Independent Monte Carlo paths require independent Gaussian increments.

Milstein

For a scalar SDE with differentiable scalar diffusion, Milstein adds the leading correction caused by varying \sigma:

X_{n+1} = X_n + b_n h + σ_n ΔW_n + ½ σ_n (∂_xσ_n) [(ΔW_n)²-h].

It reduces to Euler–Maruyama for additive noise. Multidimensional Milstein schemes can require cross-iterated stochastic integrals, so the scalar formula should not be copied blindly.

Strong error, weak error, stability, and Monte Carlo

  • Strong error compares approximate and exact paths driven by the same noise, for example E\mid X_{T}-X_{T}^h\mid. Under standard assumptions Euler–Maruyama has strong order 1/2, while scalar Milstein has order 1.
  • Weak error compares expectations \mid E[\phi (X_{T})]-E[\phi (X_{T}^h)]\mid. Euler–Maruyama usually has weak order 1 for smooth enough data.
  • Step-size stability: convergence as h→0 does not make a coarse step safe. For d X=-\lambda X d t+\sigma d W, Euler–Maruyama multiplies the old state by 1-\lambda h; the usual stability requirement \mid 1-\lambda h\mid \lt 1 gives 0\lt \lambda h\lt 2. Stiff or superlinear systems may need implicit, tamed, or adaptive methods.
  • Monte Carlo: estimate E[\phi (X_{T})] by the sample mean over M simulated paths. Sampling error is typically O(M^{-1/2}); report a standard error, and reduce discretization error separately by refining h.

8. Reverse-time SDEs

Time reversal and the score

Consider a forward diffusion

dX_t = b(X_t,t)dt + σ(X_t,t)dW_t, a=σσᵀ,

with smooth positive marginal density p_t(x). Define the increasing reverse clock s=T-t and Y_s=X_{T-s}. Under suitable regularity and boundary conditions, the reversed process is again a diffusion with drift

b_rev(x,s) = -b(x,T-s) + (∇·a)(x,T-s) + a(x,T-s)∇_x log p_{T-s}(x).

Here (∇·a)_i=Σ_j∂_{x_j}a_{ij}. If the diffusion is state-independent, the divergence term vanishes. The vector ∇_x log p_t(x) is the score: it points toward locally higher probability density.

A common equivalent convention keeps the original variable t and integrates it from T down to 0. For \sigma (x,t)=g(t)I, it is written

dX_t = [b(X_t,t)-g(t)²∇_x log p_t(X_t)]dt + g(t)dW̄_t, integrated with dt < 0 from T to 0.

The apparent sign difference comes only from the direction of the clock.

Connection to score-based diffusion models

A score-based diffusion model chooses a forward SDE that gradually corrupts data into a simple distribution, usually close to Gaussian. It trains a network s_\theta (x,t) to approximate ∇_x log p_t(x), commonly through denoising score matching. Sampling starts from the simple terminal distribution and numerically integrates the reverse-time SDE, replacing the unknown score with s_\theta. The learned score supplies exactly the density-dependent drift needed to undo the forward spreading in distribution.

Important caveats:

  • The formula assumes adequate density regularity and a nondegenerate process, or a more careful theorem for degenerate diffusions.
  • A state-dependent diffusion needs the divergence correction; the popular -g(t)² score form assumes g is independent of x.
  • The network estimates marginal scores, not individual forward noise paths. Reverse sampling matches distributions; it does not reconstruct the unique history of a noisy sample.
  • Score error, finite terminal noise, and numerical discretization all bias generated samples.

9. Girsanov/change-of-measure intuition

Girsanov’s theorem changes probabilities assigned to paths so that a drift change can be absorbed into Brownian motion. Suppose under P

dX_t = b(X_t,t)dt + σ(X_t,t)dW_t.

If the desired drift is b+\sigma u, define the exponential path weight

Z_T = exp(∫₀ᵀ u_t·dW_t - ½∫₀ᵀ ||u_t||²dt).

Under conditions such as Novikov’s condition, Z_{T} has mean one and defines d Q=Z_{\text{TdP}}. Then W_{t}^Q=W_{t}-\int _{0}^{t}u_{\text{sds}} is Brownian under Q, and

dX_t = [b(X_t,t)+σ(X_t,t)u_t]dt + σ(X_t,t)dW_t^Q.

Practically, Z_{T} is a likelihood ratio for complete paths. It supports importance sampling, drift-controlled proposals, filtering, and stochastic control: simulate under an easier drift and reweight to estimate expectations under the target law.

The precision points are important: equivalent measure changes alter drift but not quadratic variation; the new drift must lie in directions reachable through \sigma; integrability is required; and long horizons or large controls can make path weights extremely variable, producing effective sample-size collapse.

10. Applications

  • Filtering: infer a hidden diffusion state from noisy observations. Kalman–Bucy filtering covers linear-Gaussian models; nonlinear filters propagate or approximate a conditional distribution.
  • Finance: model asset prices, volatility, and rates; use Itô’s lemma for derivative dynamics and change of measure for risk-neutral pricing. Model assumptions and calibration matter as much as formal manipulation.
  • Control: choose a feedback drift to minimize expected running and terminal cost. Generators lead to Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equations; Girsanov connects controls to path measures.
  • Diffusion generative models: learn time-dependent scores and solve a reverse-time SDE to transform noise into samples.
  • Physics-informed learning: encode known stochastic dynamics, conservation structure, or noise models while learning residual drift, diffusion, closures, or unknown parameters from data.
  • Neural SDEs: parameterize b and/or \sigma with neural networks for irregular time series, latent dynamics, uncertainty-aware forecasting, and continuous-depth models. Identifiability, solver bias, and whether the learned noise is aleatoric or merely compensating for model error require scrutiny.

Review checklist

  1. Explain why Brownian increments scale as \sqrt{d} t and how that creates (d W)^{2}=d t.
  2. Apply Itô’s lemma to log X_t for geometric Brownian motion.
  3. Derive the generator from Itô’s lemma and the Fokker–Planck equation from its adjoint.
  4. Write Euler–Maruyama and distinguish strong path error from weak expectation error.
  5. State a reverse-time SDE with a clear time convention and explain the role of the score.
  6. Explain what Girsanov changes, what it cannot change, and why likelihood ratios can become unstable.
