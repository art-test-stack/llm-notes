Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) represents a changing probability distribution with weighted samples. A particle filter is the standard SMC algorithm for online inference in a state-space model.

1. Importance sampling

Target and proposal distributions

Suppose the target distribution is \pi (x) = \gamma (x) / Z, where \gamma is evaluable but the normalizing constant Z may be unknown. Draw samples x^{(i)} ~ q(x) from a proposal distribution that is easy to sample and evaluate.

Importance weights

The unnormalized and normalized importance weights are

u⁽ⁱ⁾ = γ(x⁽ⁱ⁾) / q(x⁽ⁱ⁾) w⁽ⁱ⁾ = u⁽ⁱ⁾ / Σⱼ u⁽ʲ⁾.

The ratio corrects for drawing from q rather than \pi. If Z is known, the ordinary estimator (1/N) \sum _{i} h(x^{(i)})\pi (x^{(i)})/q(x^{(i)}) is unbiased under standard integrability conditions.

Self-normalized estimator

When Z is unknown, use

Eπ[h(X)] ≈ Σᵢ w⁽ⁱ⁾ h(x⁽ⁱ⁾).

This estimator is generally biased at finite N, but consistent as N grows under suitable conditions.

Weight variance and support mismatch

A good proposal covers the target and places mass where \mid \gamma (x)h(x)\mid is large. If q(x) = 0 where the target contributes nonzero mass, importance sampling cannot recover that region: the estimator has a support mismatch. A proposal with lighter tails or a poor shape match can produce a few enormous weights, high estimator variance, and an effectively tiny sample. Weight variance is therefore a central diagnostic, not merely a numerical nuisance.

2. Sequential importance sampling

Path-space target

For latent states x_{0}:_{t} and observations y_{0}:_{t}, a state-space model factorizes as

γₜ(x₀:ₜ) = p(x₀:ₜ, y₀:ₜ) = μ(x₀) g₀(y₀ | x₀) ∏ₛ₌₁ᵗ fₛ(xₛ | xₛ₋₁) gₛ(yₛ | xₛ), πₜ(x₀:ₜ) = p(x₀:ₜ | y₀:ₜ).

Here \mu is the initial-state distribution, fₜ is the state transition, and gₜ is the observation likelihood. A sequential proposal has the form

qₜ(x₀:ₜ | y₀:ₜ) = q₀(x₀ | y₀) ∏ₛ₌₁ᵗ qₛ(xₛ | x₀:ₛ₋₁, y₀:ₛ).

Deriving the recursive weight update

Define u_{t} = \gamma _{t}/q_{t}. Both the target and proposal extend their previous path by one factor:

uₜ = γₜ(x₀:ₜ) / qₜ(x₀:ₜ | y₀:ₜ) = [γₜ₋₁(x₀:ₜ₋₁) fₜ(xₜ | xₜ₋₁) gₜ(yₜ | xₜ)] / [qₜ₋₁(x₀:ₜ₋₁ | y₀:ₜ₋₁) qₜ(xₜ | x₀:ₜ₋₁, y₀:ₜ)] = uₜ₋₁ × fₜ(xₜ | xₜ₋₁) gₜ(yₜ | xₜ) / qₜ(xₜ | x₀:ₜ₋₁, y₀:ₜ).

The initial weight is u_{0} = \mu (x_{0})g_{0}(y_{0}\mid x_{0})/q_{0}(x_{0}\mid y_{0}). This recursion avoids re-evaluating the full trajectory. Without resampling, however, the variance of path weights typically grows with time.

3. Particle filter

A particle filter interleaves sequential importance sampling with resampling. At time t:

  1. Propagate: sample each new state from q_{t}(x_{t} \mid x_{0}:_{t-1}, y_{0}:_{t}).
  2. Weight: multiply the previous weight by the transition-likelihood-to-proposal ratio.
  3. Normalize: divide weights by their sum, using log weights and log-sum-exp in practice.
  4. Estimate: approximate a filtering expectation by \sum _{i} w_{t}^{(i)} h(x_{t}^{(i)}).
  5. Resample: when weight concentration is too high, copy particles according to their weights and reset all weights to 1/N.

Pseudocode

input: observations y₀:T, particle count N, resampling threshold τ

for i = 1,...,N:
    sample x₀⁽ⁱ⁾ ~ q₀(· | y₀)
    u⁽ⁱ⁾ ← μ(x₀⁽ⁱ⁾) g₀(y₀ | x₀⁽ⁱ⁾) / q₀(x₀⁽ⁱ⁾ | y₀)
normalize w⁽ⁱ⁾ ← u⁽ⁱ⁾ / Σⱼ u⁽ʲ⁾
estimate ĥ₀ ← Σᵢ w⁽ⁱ⁾ h(x₀⁽ⁱ⁾)

for t = 1,...,T:
    for i = 1,...,N:                         # propagate
        sample xₜ⁽ⁱ⁾ ~ qₜ(· | x₀:ₜ₋₁⁽ⁱ⁾, y₀:ₜ)
        u⁽ⁱ⁾ ← w⁽ⁱ⁾ fₜ(xₜ⁽ⁱ⁾ | xₜ₋₁⁽ⁱ⁾) gₜ(yₜ | xₜ⁽ⁱ⁾)
                    / qₜ(xₜ⁽ⁱ⁾ | x₀:ₜ₋₁⁽ⁱ⁾, y₀:ₜ)  # weight
    normalize w⁽ⁱ⁾ ← u⁽ⁱ⁾ / Σⱼ u⁽ʲ⁾
    estimate ĥₜ ← Σᵢ w⁽ⁱ⁾ h(xₜ⁽ⁱ⁾)
    if 1 / Σᵢ (w⁽ⁱ⁾)² < τN:                # resample adaptively
        sample ancestor indices a₁:N from Categorical(w⁽¹⁾,...,w⁽ᴺ⁾)
        replace paths x₀:ₜ⁽ⁱ⁾ ← x₀:ₜ⁽ᵃⁱ⁾
        set w⁽ⁱ⁾ ← 1/N for every i

return filtering estimates ĥ₀:T and retained particle genealogy

With the bootstrap proposal q_{t} = f_{t}, transition and proposal cancel, so the incremental weight is simply gₜ(yₜ|xₜ).

4. Effective sample size

For normalized weights, the common diagnostic is

ESS = 1 / Σᵢ (w⁽ⁱ⁾)².

ESS ranges from 1 when one particle has all the weight to N when weights are uniform. It is a heuristic measure of weight concentration, not a literal count of independent samples and not a guarantee of state-space diversity.

Adaptive resampling triggers when ESS \lt \tau N, often with a problem-dependent \tau such as 0.5. Resampling too rarely permits weight collapse; resampling too often adds sampling noise and accelerates genealogical collapse.

Worked calculation

For four normalized weights (0.50, 0.30, 0.15, 0.05),

Σᵢ (w⁽ⁱ⁾)² = 0.50² + 0.30² + 0.15² + 0.05² = 0.25 + 0.09 + 0.0225 + 0.0025 = 0.365 ESS = 1 / 0.365 ≈ 2.74.

The weighted sample carries roughly the weight balance of 2.74 equally weighted particles out of four. A threshold of 0.5N = 2 would not trigger resampling, whereas 0.75N = 3 would.

5. Resampling methods

MethodMechanismVariance and implementation trade-offs
MultinomialDraw N independent ancestor indices from the categorical weights.Simplest and easy to parallelize, but usually has the highest resampling variance among these choices.
StratifiedDraw one uniform point inside each of N equal subintervals of [0,1), then invert the cumulative weights.Lower variance than multinomial; requires a cumulative sum and N random draws.
SystematicDraw one random offset, then use N evenly spaced points through the cumulative weights.Fast, low random-number cost, and often low variance, though offspring counts are dependent and its ordering-dependent theory is subtler.
ResidualAssign ⌊\text{Nw}^{(i)}⌋ copies deterministically, then sample the remaining slots from residual weights.Reduces randomness for large weights; bookkeeping is slightly more involved and the residual step still needs another scheme.

All are unbiased in the sense that a particle's expected offspring count is \text{Nw}^{(i)}. Stratified, systematic, and residual resampling usually improve on multinomial variance, but no resampler fixes a bad proposal or an uninformative particle cloud.

6. Degeneracy and impoverishment

  • Weight collapse: after repeated updates, almost all weight lies on very few particles.
  • Path degeneracy: current particles may differ while their stored trajectories coalesce onto a small number of early ancestors; long-horizon smoothing then becomes unreliable.
  • Sample impoverishment: resampling duplicates high-weight states and removes low-weight ones, reducing diversity, especially when process noise is small.
  • High-dimensional observations: likelihood ratios become extremely concentrated, so the required particle count may grow prohibitively.
  • Poor proposals: proposing without using the newest observation sends particles into regions that the likelihood immediately rejects.

More particles delay failure but do not change its cause. Better proposals, conditional structure, rejuvenation moves, and model redesign are often more effective.

7. Better proposals

  • Bootstrap proposal: q_{t}(x_{t}\mid x_{t-1},y_{t})=f_{t}(x_{t}\mid x_{t-1}). It is cheap and general, but ignores yₜ when proposing.
  • Locally optimal proposal: q_{t}(x_{t}\mid x_{t-1},y_{t})=p(x_{t}\mid x_{t-1},y_{t}). It minimizes the conditional variance of the incremental weight; the remaining weight is proportional to p(y_{t}\mid x_{t-1}). It is often unavailable in closed form.
  • Auxiliary particle filter: uses a look-ahead score, commonly predictive likelihood, to select promising ancestors before propagation.
  • Learned proposals: train a network to map observations and particle context to proposal parameters. They can exploit complex observations but must generalize across regimes and still permit valid density evaluation or correction.
  • Rao–Blackwellization: analytically integrate a conditionally tractable part of the state, such as a linear-Gaussian submodel handled by a Kalman filter, and sample only the nonlinear remainder. This reduces Monte Carlo variance at the cost of model-specific derivation.

8. Smoothing

Filtering estimates p(x_{t}\mid y_{0}:_{t}) using observations available by time t. Smoothing estimates p(x_{t}\mid y_{0}:T) for T \gt t, allowing later observations to revise earlier states.

  • Ancestor tracing: select a terminal particle and follow its stored parent indices backward. It is cheap, but path degeneracy can leave few distinct early trajectories.
  • Backward simulation: after a forward particle filter, sample backward using weights proportional to the forward filtering weight times f_{t+1}(x_{t+1}\mid x_{t}). This can recover more diverse trajectories than tracing only recorded ancestors.
  • Fixed-lag smoothing: estimate xₜ₋ℓ once observations through t arrive, assuming sufficiently distant future observations add little information. It bounds memory and delay but introduces lag-dependent bias.

9. Parameter learning

  • Particle EM: use particle smoothing to approximate expected complete-data sufficient statistics in the E-step, then update model parameters in the M-step. Monte Carlo error and path degeneracy can destabilize the updates.
  • Particle marginal Metropolis–Hastings (PMMH): propose parameters, run a particle filter to obtain a nonnegative unbiased likelihood estimate, and place that estimate in the Metropolis–Hastings ratio. Under standard conditions, the extended-state chain targets the exact parameter posterior despite noisy likelihood estimates; excessive log-likelihood variance causes poor mixing.
  • SMC²: maintain particles over parameters, each carrying an inner particle filter over latent states. Tempering or sequential observations update parameter weights, while PMCMC moves rejuvenate them. It is principled but computationally expensive.
  • Differentiable particle filters: learn dynamics, likelihoods, or proposals end to end. Reparameterized propagation can carry pathwise gradients, but discrete resampling and ancestor choices break ordinary differentiation. Score-function estimators are noisy; soft-resampling relaxations or stopped gradients can be biased; long sequences also suffer weight and gradient collapse.

10. Evaluation

MeasureWhat to report
ESSIts trajectory over time, minimum values, and resampling frequency; ESS alone does not measure state accuracy.
Log-likelihood estimateThe particle filter estimate of the marginal likelihood, its bias after taking logs, and its variance across independent runs.
State RMSEError of filtering and smoothing estimates against known latent states, broken down by state component and horizon.
CoverageEmpirical frequency with which nominal credible intervals or regions contain the true state or parameter, together with interval width.
Number of particlesAccuracy and stability as N increases; compare methods at equal particle count and equal compute.
RuntimeLatency, throughput, memory, hardware, and the cost of proposal, likelihood, resampling, and smoothing operations.
Repeated-run varianceVariation across independent random seeds in likelihood estimates, RMSE, coverage, and runtime; include uncertainty bars rather than one favorable run.

A credible evaluation varies particle count, proposal, resampler, and resampling threshold; reports accuracy–runtime trade-offs; and repeats every stochastic configuration. When ground-truth states are unavailable, predictive checks, held-out likelihood, calibration, and task-specific downstream metrics become especially important.
