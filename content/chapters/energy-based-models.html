Energy-based models (EBMs) assign a scalar energy to each configuration. Low energy means high compatibility; probability is obtained by exponentiating the negative energy. This gives a flexible modelling language, but learning and sampling are coupled because low-energy configurations must be found to normalize the model and compute likelihood gradients.

1. Energy formulation

For a state x, an EBM with parameters \theta defines

p_\theta (x) = \operatorname{exp}(-E_\theta (x)) / Z_\theta,

where:

  • Energy E_\theta (x) is a learned scalar. Only relative energies determine probabilities: adding the same constant to every energy leaves p_\theta unchanged.
  • Partition function Z_\theta = \int \operatorname{exp}(-E_\theta (x)) d\mu (x) normalizes relative to the chosen base measure \mu (counting measure gives the discrete sum). The exact condition is integrability: \operatorname{exp}(-E) must have finite, nonzero integral. Sufficient tail growth is a common way to ensure this on unbounded Euclidean domains, but it is not a universal necessity on finite, discrete, or bounded domains.

A normalized model exposes a tractable probability whose total mass is known to be one, as in an autoregressive model or a normalizing flow. An unnormalized model exposes only q\theta (x) = \operatorname{exp}(-E_\theta (x)); probabilities and likelihoods require the usually intractable Z_\theta. EBMs are often trained and used through this unnormalized representation.

For a conditional EBM,

p_\theta(y\mid x)=\frac{\exp\!\bigl(-E_\theta(x,y)\bigr)}{Z_\theta(x)}, with Z_\theta(x)=\sum_{y'}\exp\!\bigl(-E_\theta(x,y')\bigr).

The conditional partition function can be easy when y ranges over a small label set, yet intractable for sequences or combinatorial structures. Conditioning therefore does not remove normalization; it changes the space over which normalization is performed.

2. Maximum-likelihood gradient

For one observation x, start from

\log p_\theta(x)=-E_\theta(x)-\log Z_\theta.

Differentiate the partition function explicitly:

\nabla_\theta\log Z_\theta=\frac{1}{Z_\theta}\nabla_\theta\int\exp\!\bigl(-E_\theta(x')\bigr)\,dx'
=-\frac{1}{Z_\theta}\int\exp\!\bigl(-E_\theta(x')\bigr)\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x')\,dx'
=-\mathbb{E}_{x'\sim p_\theta}\!\left[\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x')\right].

Substitution gives the log-likelihood gradient:

\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)=-\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x)+\mathbb{E}_{x'\sim p_\theta}\!\left[\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x')\right].

Averaging over data gives

\nabla_\theta\mathcal L=-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}}\!\left[\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta}\!\left[\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x)\right].

  • The positive phase, -\mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{data}}}[\nabla E], lowers the energy of observed data under gradient ascent on log likelihood.
  • The negative phase, +\mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[\nabla E], raises the energy of states sampled from the model.

For gradient descent on negative log likelihood, both signs reverse: \nabla\mathrm{NLL}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{data}}[\nabla E]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{model}}[\nabla E]. Naming conventions sometimes refer to the phases by their effect rather than their written sign, so stating the optimized objective avoids ambiguity.

The data expectation is easy to estimate with a minibatch. The model expectation requires samples from the current normalized model. Computing Z_\theta exactly entails integrating or summing over the entire state space, while approximate sampling needs a Markov chain that itself uses the learned energy. This is the central maximum-likelihood training difficulty.

3. MCMC sampling

Langevin dynamics

For differentiable energies over continuous x, the score is \nabla_x\log p_\theta(x)=-\nabla_xE_\theta(x). The unadjusted Langevin algorithm (ULA) iterates

x_{t+1}=x_t-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\nabla_xE_\theta(x_t)+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\xi_t, where \xi_t\sim\mathcal N(0,I).

The gradient drifts toward lower energy and Gaussian noise prevents pure optimization, enabling distributional exploration. Equivalent conventions use \varepsilon for the drift and \sqrt{2\varepsilon } for the noise; the ratio must be consistent.

Step size controls a bias–mixing trade-off. Large steps explore faster but discretize the diffusion poorly and can become unstable; small steps reduce discretization bias but require more transitions. ULA has finite-step stationary bias.

Metropolis-adjusted Langevin (MALA) proposes a Langevin step and accepts it using a Metropolis–Hastings correction based on both target-density and asymmetric proposal ratios. The unknown partition function cancels in the acceptance ratio. MALA is asymptotically exact for a fixed target, but rejections and acceptance-ratio calculations add cost; during rapidly changing neural-EBM training, practitioners often use unadjusted short-run chains instead.

Discrete and persistent chains

Gibbs sampling is relevant when variables are discrete and each conditional p(xi | x-i) can be normalized cheaply. It updates one variable or block at a time. Restricted Boltzmann machines are a classic favorable case because alternating hidden and visible conditionals factorize. General neural energies may not provide tractable conditionals.

Mixing asks how quickly a chain forgets its initialization and moves between important modes. High energy barriers, separated modes, and high dimension can trap local samplers, creating correlated negatives and poor mode coverage. More steps do not guarantee mixing; diagnostics and multiple initializations matter.

Persistent chains retain their final states across parameter updates instead of restarting from noise. A replay buffer stores past negative samples and initializes new chains from that reservoir, often mixing persistent states with fresh random starts. These mechanisms amortize burn-in, but stale or mode-collapsed buffers can make the negative phase unrepresentative as the energy changes.

4. Contrastive divergence

CD-k initializes a Markov chain at a data point, applies k transitions, and replaces the exact model expectation with the endpoint distribution. The difference KL(p_{\mathrm{data}} \lVert p_\theta ) - KL(\text{pk} \rVert p_\theta ) provides useful intuition when the transition kernel leaves the model invariant, but the practical update normally stops gradients through the parameter-dependent finite-step distribution pk. It is therefore not generally the exact gradient of that KL difference, or of any fixed scalar objective.

CD-k is cheap and its negatives expose nearby defects around data, but it is a biased likelihood-gradient estimator for finite k. It usually ignores the dependence of the sampling distribution on \theta, and short chains need not represent the equilibrium model.

Persistent contrastive divergence (PCD) updates a bank of chains continuously across minibatches. If learning is slow relative to mixing, persistent states can better track the model distribution than chains restarted at data. That condition often fails for expressive, rapidly changing neural energies.

5. Alternative objectives

Several objectives avoid differentiating a global partition function, but they trade it for derivatives, corruption choices, auxiliary noise, or local normalization.

ObjectiveCore ideaTrade-off
Score matchingFit \nabla_x\log p_\theta(x)=-\nabla_xE_\theta(x) to the data score. Integration by parts yields a loss proportional to \mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{data}}\!\left[\tfrac12\lVert\nabla_xE\rVert^2-\Delta_xE\right], without Z.Requires smooth continuous densities and second derivatives or estimators of the trace of the Hessian.
Denoising score matchingCorrupt data with known noise and regress the model score of the noisy density toward the corruption score, for Gaussian noise typically -(\widetilde{x} - x) / \sigma ^{2}.Avoids clean-data score access and is scalable, but learns noise-level-dependent scores; the corruption schedule matters.
Noise-contrastive estimation (NCE)Train a classifier to distinguish data from samples drawn from a known noise distribution. The classifier uses the log unnormalized density, noise density, and often a learned log-normalizer.Consistent under suitable conditions, but efficiency depends strongly on overlap and the number and quality of noise samples.
Ratio matchingFor discrete data, match ratios or conditional odds between neighboring states, causing the global normalizer to cancel.Avoids global normalization but depends on a useful neighborhood structure and can require many local comparisons.
PseudolikelihoodMaximize \sum_i\log p_\theta(x_i\mid x_{-i}); each conditional normalizes only over values of one variable.Often tractable and statistically consistent in classical settings, but it optimizes local conditionals rather than joint likelihood and can miss global structure at finite sample size.

6. Connection to score models

For any differentiable EBM,

s_\theta(x)=\nabla_x\log p_\theta(x)=-\nabla_xE_\theta(x),

because Z_\theta does not depend on x. A scalar energy therefore induces a conservative score field. Conversely, an arbitrary learned vector field need not be the gradient of a globally consistent scalar energy.

Diffusion models estimate a family of scores s_\theta(x_t,t)\approx\nabla_{x_t}\log p_t(x_t) for progressively noised distributions. Sampling reverses the known forward noising process through an SDE, ODE, or discrete solver.

AspectExplicit EBM trainingDiffusion / time-indexed score training
Learned objectUsually one scalar energy at the data distributionA score or noise predictor across many noise levels
Training negativesLikelihood training needs model samples for the negative phaseDenoising targets come from directly corrupted data; no equilibrium negative-phase chain
SamplingTypically MCMC on a fixed energy landscapeStarts from a known high-noise distribution and follows a scheduled reverse process
NormalizationThe partition function is generally unknownLikelihood may be obtainable through probability-flow or variational machinery, but is not an explicit normalized density from one scalar energy
Field structureThe score is exactly an energy gradientA neural score field is not necessarily parameterized as the gradient of a scalar

Thus diffusion training is related through score matching, but it is not simply maximum-likelihood training of a conventional single-energy EBM.

7. Conditional and structured EBMs

  • Classification: assign one energy E(x,y) per class and compute a softmax over -E(x,y). Cross-entropy is exactly conditional maximum likelihood when all classes are enumerated.
  • Structured prediction: score entire candidate structures such as trees, segmentations, or label grids. Dynamic programming is possible when the energy factorizes; otherwise approximate search and normalization are required.
  • Sequence scoring: assign a sequence-level energy without committing to a left-to-right factorization. This permits global features but makes decoding and normalization over all sequences difficult.
  • Retrieval and reranking: treat E(\text{query}, \text{item}) as negative compatibility. Softmax over a sampled candidate set gives a useful contrastive objective, but it is not the globally normalized probability over the full corpus unless the full denominator is represented or corrected.
  • Joint input–output energies: define E(x,y) for compatible pairs. The same function can support conditional prediction, joint modelling, or constraint injection, but each choice implies a different partition function and training objective.

8. Modern neural EBMs

Deep networks make energies expressive but also make their landscapes difficult to sample. Practical controls include:

  • Spectral normalization or regularization: constrain weight norms, input gradients, or curvature so that MCMC drift does not become arbitrarily steep. This can improve stability without guaranteeing good mixing.
  • Energy scale: an unconstrained rescaling changes concentration and Langevin gradients. Energies that are too steep trap chains; energies that are too flat fail to separate data from negatives. Temperature and gradient penalties must be treated as part of the model.
  • Negative-sample quality: easy or stale negatives let the model win without learning the true data boundary. Better chains, hard-negative mining, and buffer refreshes help, though mined negatives may change the objective.
  • Mode coverage: local chains can repeatedly visit a subset of modes. The model may assign unseen low-energy regions that training never discovers, sometimes called spurious energy wells.
  • Training instability: the sampler and energy co-evolve. A parameter update can invalidate persistent samples; poor negatives then distort the next update, creating a feedback loop. Monitor energy magnitudes, gradient norms, chain diversity, and buffer age rather than loss alone.

9. Evaluation

  • Sample quality: inspect precision and recall or domain-appropriate quality/diversity metrics, not only visually attractive cherry-picked chains.
  • Energy separation: compare energies for held-out data, generated samples, corrupted inputs, and strong negatives. Separation is sampler- and negative-set-dependent and does not by itself prove normalized density quality.
  • Calibration: for conditional predictions with tractable class normalization, use reliability curves, proper scoring rules, and selective-risk analysis. Raw energies are not probabilities and their scale is not calibrated confidence.
  • OOD detection limitations: low energy or high likelihood does not reliably imply in-distribution semantics. Background statistics, volume effects, architecture, and training negatives can give OOD inputs deceptively favorable scores. Evaluate multiple real OOD sets and near-boundary shifts.
  • MCMC diagnostics: run multiple chains; report trace plots, autocorrelation, effective sample size, acceptance rate for MALA, transition statistics, mode occupancy, and sensitivity to initialization and chain length.
  • Likelihood estimation difficulty: exact likelihood needs Z. Annealed importance sampling, sequential Monte Carlo, or thermodynamic integration can estimate it, but results depend on intermediate paths, effective sample size, and mixing. Report uncertainty and sanity-check on tractable cases.

10. Worked example: exact finite-state EBM

Let x ∈ {0,1,2} and E_\theta (x) = \theta x. Choose \theta = \operatorname{log} 2. The unnormalized weights are

\operatorname{exp}(-E(0)) = 1, \operatorname{exp}(-E(1)) = 1/2, and \operatorname{exp}(-E(2)) = 1/4.

Therefore

Z_\theta = 1 + 1/2 + 1/4 = 7/4,

and the exact normalized probabilities are

p(0) = 4/7, p(1) = 2/7, p(2) = 1/7.

Suppose the training set contains one observation \text{xdata} = 2. Since \partial E_\theta (x)/\partial \theta = x, the positive-phase term in the log-likelihood gradient is -\text{xdata} = -2. The exact model expectation is

\text{Ep}\theta [x] = 0(4/7) + 1(2/7) + 2(1/7) = 4/7.

Hence

\partial \operatorname{log} p_\theta (2) / \partial \theta = -2 + 4/7 = -10/7.

Equivalently, the negative-log-likelihood gradient is +10/7. Gradient descent therefore decreases \theta, which lowers the energy of the observed high-x state relative to the other states and raises its probability. In a large state space, the simple exact sum producing 4/7 would be replaced by the difficult model-expectation estimate.

Review summary

  1. An EBM becomes probabilistic through \operatorname{exp}(-E) and a partition function.
  2. Maximum likelihood lowers data energy and raises model-sample energy; the latter requires sampling from the current model.
  3. Langevin dynamics combines energy-gradient drift with noise, but finite step size and poor mixing bias practical negative samples.
  4. Contrastive divergence, score matching, NCE, ratio matching, and pseudolikelihood avoid or approximate the exact negative phase in different ways.
  5. Diffusion models learn time-indexed noisy-data scores and have a different training and sampling construction from a single explicit EBM.
