Probabilistic state-space models describe a hidden process that evolves through time and generates noisy observations. Hidden Markov models (HMMs) use discrete hidden states; linear Gaussian state-space models use continuous Gaussian states. Both share the same predict–update logic.

1. Latent Markov models

Let z_t be the hidden state and x_t the observation at time t. The first-order Markov and conditional-independence assumptions are

p(z_t | z_1,…,z_{t-1}) = p(z_t | z_{t-1}) p(x_t | z_1,…,z_t,x_1,…,x_{t-1}) = p(x_t | z_t).

A model is specified by an initial distribution p(z_1), transition distribution p(z_t | z_{t-1}), and emission distribution p(x_t | z_t). Its joint distribution factorizes as

p(z_1:T, x_1:T) = p(z_1) p(x_1 | z_1) ∏_{t=2}^T p(z_t | z_{t-1}) p(x_t | z_t).

The main inference tasks are filtering p(z_t | x_1:t), prediction p(z_{t+k} | x_1:t), smoothing p(z_t | x_1:T), sequence likelihood p(x_1:T), and path decoding.

2. Hidden Markov Models

In an HMM, z_t ∈ {1,…,K}. Write the initial probabilities as π_i = p(z_1=i), the row-stochastic transition matrix as A_ij = p(z_t=j | z_{t-1}=i), and the emission probability or density as b_i(x) = p(x_t=x | z_t=i).

For a fixed state path,

p(z_1:T, x_1:T) = π_{z_1} b_{z_1}(x_1) ∏_{t=2}^T A_{z_{t-1},z_t} b_{z_t}(x_t).

The sequence likelihood marginalizes all K^T paths:

p(x_1:T) = ∑_{z_1:T} p(z_1:T, x_1:T).

Direct enumeration is exponential. The forward algorithm performs the same sum in O(TK²).

3. Forward algorithm

Define the unnormalized forward message

α_t(j) = p(x_1:t, z_t=j).

The base case follows from the initial and emission distributions:

α_1(j) = π_j b_j(x_1).

For the recursion, insert the previous state and use the Markov assumptions:

α_t(j) = p(x_t | z_t=j) ∑_i p(x_1:t-1, z_{t-1}=i) p(z_t=j | z_{t-1}=i) = b_j(x_t) ∑_i α_{t-1}(i) A_ij.

The likelihood is p(x_1:T)=∑_j α_T(j). Each of T steps evaluates K² transitions, so time is O(TK²); likelihood-only memory is O(K), or O(TK) when all messages are retained for smoothing.

Scaling and log space

Products of many probabilities underflow. One scaled implementation computes

α̃_t(j) = b_j(x_t) ∑_i α̂_{t-1}(i) A_ij c_t = ∑_j α̃_t(j) α̂_t(j) = α̃_t(j) / c_t log p(x_1:T) = ∑_{t=1}^T log c_t,

with α̂_0 interpreted through the usual initialization. Alternatively, store ℓ_t(j)=log α_t(j) and use

ℓ_t(j) = log b_j(x_t) + logsumexp_i(ℓ_{t-1}(i) + log A_ij).

Worked numerical example

Consider states R (rainy) and S (sunny), observations U (umbrella) and N (no umbrella), and the sequence U,U,N:

π = [0.6, 0.4] next R next S A = R [0.7, 0.3] S [0.4, 0.6] U N b_R 0.9 0.1 b_S 0.2 0.8

The forward table is

4. Backward algorithm and smoothing

Define the backward message

β_t(i) = p(x_{t+1:T} | z_t=i).

Nothing remains to observe after T, so β_T(i)=1. Inserting z_{t+1} gives the backward recursion

β_t(i) = ∑_j p(z_{t+1}=j | z_t=i) p(x_{t+1} | z_{t+1}=j) p(x_{t+2:T} | z_{t+1}=j) = ∑_j A_ij b_j(x_{t+1}) β_{t+1}(j).

Combining past evidence in α with future evidence in β yields the smoothed one-state posterior

γ_t(i) = p(z_t=i | x_1:T) = α_t(i) β_t(i) / p(x_1:T).

The pairwise transition posterior, needed by EM, is

ξ_t(i,j) = p(z_t=i,z_{t+1}=j | x_1:T) = α_t(i) A_ij b_j(x_{t+1}) β_{t+1}(j) / p(x_1:T).

With scaled forward messages, backward messages must use the matching convention, for example β̂_t(i)=∑_j A_ij b_j(x_{t+1})β̂_{t+1}(j)/c_{t+1}.

For the example, β_3=[1,1], β_2=[0.31,0.52], and β_1=[0.2265,0.1740]. The identity

∑_i α_1(i)β_1(i) = .54·.2265 + .08·.174 = .13623

checks the likelihood. At time 2,

γ_2(R) = .369·.31/.13623 ≈ .8397 γ_2(S) = .042·.52/.13623 ≈ .1603.

5. Viterbi decoding

Viterbi finds the single most likely joint path

z* = argmax_{z_1:T} p(z_1:T, x_1:T).

Replace the forward sum by a maximum. Define δ_t(j) as the best joint probability of any partial path ending at j and ψ_t(j) as its predecessor:

δ_1(j) = π_j b_j(x_1) δ_t(j) = b_j(x_t) max_i[δ_{t-1}(i) A_ij] ψ_t(j) = argmax_i[δ_{t-1}(i) A_ij].

Choose z_T*=argmax_j δ_T(j), then backtrack with z_t*=ψ_{t+1}(z_{t+1}*). Use log probabilities in practice. Time is O(TK²).

In the example, δ_2=[0.3402,0.0324] and δ_3=[0.023814,0.081648], giving the Viterbi path R,R,S.

Viterbi versus posterior decoding

  • Viterbi decoding maximizes the probability of the whole path and always returns a transition-consistent path.
  • Marginal posterior decoding chooses argmax_i γ_t(i) independently at each time. It minimizes expected per-time-step classification error, but the combined choices need not form the most probable path and can even include an impossible transition when some A_ij=0.

They happen to agree on R,R,S in this example; that is not true in general because max and marginalization answer different questions.

6. Baum–Welch / EM

When states are unobserved, Baum–Welch applies expectation–maximization to the HMM likelihood.

E-step

Run forward–backward under the current parameters. The responsibilities γ_t(i) are expected state indicators, and ξ_t(i,j) are expected transition indicators. Thus

expected visits to i = ∑_t γ_t(i) expected transitions i→j = ∑_{t=1}^{T-1} ξ_t(i,j).

M-step

Maximize the expected complete-data log-likelihood. For a categorical HMM, normalized expected counts give

π_i(new) = γ_1(i) A_ij(new) = [∑_{t=1}^{T-1} ξ_t(i,j)] / [∑_{t=1}^{T-1} γ_t(i)] b_i(v)(new) = [∑_{t=1}^{T} γ_t(i) 1{x_t=v}] / [∑_{t=1}^{T} γ_t(i)].

For multiple sequences, sum the expected counts across sequences before normalizing. Other emission families use their weighted maximum-likelihood updates; priors or pseudocounts can regularize sparse counts.

EM does not guarantee the global optimum. It can converge to local optima, degenerate states, or different label permutations. Use several initializations, sensible scale estimates, regularization, held-out likelihood, and convergence checks. Increasing training likelihood alone does not establish a meaningful latent interpretation.

7. Linear Gaussian state-space models

A controlled linear Gaussian model is

z_t = F_t z_{t-1} + B_t u_t + w_t, w_t ~ N(0,Q_t) x_t = H_t z_t + D_t u_t + v_t, v_t ~ N(0,R_t),

where process noise w_t represents uncertain dynamics and measurement noise v_t represents sensor error. Assume they are independent over time and mutually independent, with z_0 ~ N(m_0,P_0).

Kalman prediction

If z_{t-1}|x_1:t-1 ~ N(m_{t-1|t-1},P_{t-1|t-1}), an affine transformation plus independent Gaussian noise gives

m_{t|t-1} = F_t m_{t-1|t-1} + B_t u_t P_{t|t-1} = F_t P_{t-1|t-1} F_tᵀ + Q_t.

Innovation and update

The predicted observation, innovation, and innovation covariance are

x̂_t = H_t m_{t|t-1} + D_t u_t r_t = x_t - x̂_t S_t = H_t P_{t|t-1} H_tᵀ + R_t.

Before observing x_t, the state and innovation are jointly Gaussian with cross-covariance P_{t|t-1}H_tᵀ. Conditioning that joint Gaussian gives the Kalman gain and posterior:

K_t = P_{t|t-1} H_tᵀ S_t⁻¹ m_{t|t} = m_{t|t-1} + K_t r_t P_{t|t} = P_{t|t-1} - K_t S_t K_tᵀ = (I-K_t H_t) P_{t|t-1}.

This is the prediction/update derivation: prediction propagates the previous Gaussian through the transition; update is exact Gaussian conditioning. Numerically, solve linear systems instead of forming S_t⁻¹. The Joseph form

P_{t|t} = (I-K_tH_t)P_{t|t-1}(I-K_tH_t)ᵀ + K_tR_tK_tᵀ

better preserves symmetry and positive semidefiniteness in finite precision.

The innovation is both the correction signal and a diagnostic: under a correct model it is zero-mean with covariance S_t. Its Gaussian density also supplies each term of the data log-likelihood.

Filtering and smoothing

Kalman filtering computes p(z_t|x_1:t) online. Rauch–Tung–Striebel smoothing uses all observations. After a forward filtering pass, run backward for t=T-1,…,0:

J_t = P_{t|t} F_{t+1}ᵀ P_{t+1|t}⁻¹ m_{t|T} = m_{t|t} + J_t(m_{t+1|T} - m_{t+1|t}) P_{t|T} = P_{t|t} + J_t(P_{t+1|T} - P_{t+1|t})J_tᵀ.

As with HMM forward–backward, filtering carries past evidence forward and smoothing propagates future evidence backward.

8. Nonlinear/non-Gaussian extensions

  • Extended Kalman filter (EKF): linearizes nonlinear transition and observation functions with Jacobians around the current estimate. It is cheap but can be inaccurate or unstable under strong nonlinearity.
  • Unscented Kalman filter (UKF): propagates a deterministic set of sigma points through nonlinear functions, matching moments without explicit Jacobians. It often captures curvature better than an EKF but costs more.
  • Particle filter: represents the filtering distribution by weighted samples. It handles non-Gaussian and multimodal posteriors, but importance weights degenerate; resampling and good proposals are central. Cost grows with the number of particles and state difficulty.
  • Switching linear dynamical system: a discrete Markov mode selects among linear Gaussian dynamics. It combines regime changes with continuous states, but exact inference grows combinatorially, motivating mixture pruning, variational methods, or particles.

9. Identifiability and observability

  • State-label permutation: permuting HMM state labels and consistently permuting π, rows/columns of A, and emissions leaves the observation distribution unchanged. Labels have no intrinsic meaning.
  • Parameter identifiability: distinct parameter settings can induce the same or nearly the same observation law. Redundant states, weakly separated emissions, similarity transforms of continuous latent coordinates, and insufficient excitation make interpretation difficult.
  • Observability: for a time-invariant linear system, the state is observable when 𝒪=[H;HF;…;HF^{n-1}] has full column rank. This is a structural property of (F,H), distinct from low-noise or statistically precise estimation.
  • Missing data: if x_t is wholly missing and missingness is ignorable, omit the emission/update at t and perform prediction only. For partially missing vectors, update with the observed components of H_t, R_t, and x_t. Non-ignorable missingness requires a model of the missingness mechanism.
  • Irregular sampling: transition dynamics must depend on the elapsed time Δt. For continuous-time linear dynamics, F(Δt)=exp(AΔt) and the process covariance is the corresponding noise integral. Reusing a fixed-step transition silently changes the model.

10. Neural state-space models

Probabilistic neural state-space models retain latent random variables but replace fixed transition or emission maps with learned functions, for example

p_θ(z_t | z_{t-1},u_t) = N(μ_θ(z_{t-1},u_t), diag σ²_θ(z_{t-1},u_t)) p_θ(x_t | z_t) = observation family with parameters g_θ(z_t).

Deep Markov models use neural parameterizations of this kind. Because the posterior over an entire latent trajectory is generally intractable, an inference network q_φ(z_1:T|x_1:T) is trained variationally by maximizing an evidence lower bound:

log p_θ(x_1:T) ≥ E_{q_φ}[log p_θ(x_1:T,z_1:T) - log q_φ(z_1:T|x_1:T)].

The inference model may be filtering or smoothing and must respect the desired information pattern. Flexible neural functions do not remove concerns about posterior collapse, calibration, identifiability, or extrapolation.

Probabilistic models versus Mamba/S4-style neural SSM layers

QuestionProbabilistic state-space modelS4/Mamba-style sequence layer
Primary objectA normalized joint distribution over latent states and observationsA deterministic learned sequence-to-sequence computation
State uncertaintyExplicit posterior such as p(z_t|x_1:t) or an approximationUsually no posterior distribution over the recurrent hidden activation
Noise modelExplicit process and observation noiseNot required; training loss may be discriminative or next-token likelihood
Core operationPrediction, Bayesian update, marginalization, and smoothingStructured recurrence/convolution or selective scan for efficient representation learning
Typical goalLatent-state estimation, forecasting, uncertainty, and system identificationEfficient long-sequence modelling with compact recurrent state

The shared phrase state-space model reflects a related recurrence vocabulary, not equivalence. S4 begins from structured linear state-space dynamics, and Mamba makes parts of its recurrence input-dependent, but their hidden activations are not automatically physical states, probabilistic latent variables, or Bayesian filtering distributions. To obtain those meanings, the model must specify a generative distribution and an inference procedure.

Common pitfalls

  • confusing p(z_t|x_1:t) filtering with p(z_t|x_1:T) smoothing;
  • calling Viterbi states the per-time posterior modes;
  • running probability-space recursions without scaling or log space;
  • interpreting permutable latent labels as uniquely identified concepts;
  • using a fixed transition matrix under irregular sampling;
  • forming covariance inverses explicitly in Kalman code;
  • equating a neural sequence layer's hidden activation with a probabilistic posterior state.
