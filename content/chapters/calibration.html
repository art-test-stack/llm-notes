Learning objectives

You should be able to distinguish hallucination, grounding, confidence, and calibration; define and diagnose probabilistic calibration; evaluate abstention and evidence support; construct task-level confidence from imperfect LLM signals; and state what uncertainty and conformal methods do and do not guarantee.

Technical framing

The applied engineering technical framing emphasizes deterministic evaluation, data semantics, distribution shift, and the need to separate a hypothesis from a validated target. The training-method note recommends stage-specific evaluation rather than one universal metric. The Transformer-family note highlights horizon-dependent error, temporal leakage, calibration, interval coverage, and retrieval failures such as irrelevant passages, conflicting sources, and injected context.

These observations imply that confidence must be evaluated on the same unit and distribution as the downstream decision.

Detailed treatment

Core distinctions

  • Hallucination: a generated claim is unsupported, contradicted, fabricated, or incorrectly synthesized.
  • Grounding: the answer is supported by a specified evidence set.
  • Confidence: the system's stated or derived belief in an answer.
  • Calibration: among predictions assigned probability p, approximately a fraction p should be correct.

Grounding does not prove that the evidence itself is true. A perfectly grounded answer can reproduce a false source. Conversely, an answer can be factually correct without being grounded in the context it cites.

Calibration definitions

Binary calibration

For a binary outcome Y ∈ {0,1} and predicted probability P, perfect calibration means Pr(Y = 1 | P = p) = p for every relevant p. Operationally, among many cases scored near 0.8, about 80% should be positive. This is a population property; finite samples only estimate it.

Multiclass calibration

For class-probability vector p = (p₁,…,pK), full multiclass calibration requires Pr(Y = k | P = p) = pₖ for every class k and probability vector p. This is stronger than top-label confidence calibration, which asks only whether Pr(Y = argmax(P) | max(P) = c) = c.

  • Marginal calibration: the average forecast for each class matches its observed base rate, E[Pₖ] = Pr(Y = k). It can hold while individual predictions are badly calibrated.
  • Classwise calibration: for every class separately, Pr(Y = k | Pₖ = p) = p. Pooling all classes or examining only the winning class can hide a severe error for a rare class.

Sequence and task-level confidence

For generation, first define the event whose probability matters: “the entire answer is correct,” “all material claims are supported,” “the program passes hidden tests,” or “the tool action succeeds.” A task-level confidence score is calibrated only against that same binary or graded outcome. Token calibration does not imply sequence, claim, or task calibration because errors are dependent and many surface forms can express the same answer.

Calibration under distribution shift

Calibration is distribution-specific. A score calibrated on one domain, language, time period, retrieval stack, prompt template, or decoding policy need not remain calibrated after any of those change. Report in-distribution and shifted slices separately, monitor score and outcome drift, and recalibrate with fresh labeled deployment-like data. Without timely labels, drift detection is only a warning; it does not demonstrate maintained calibration.

Calibration metrics

Use complementary metrics. Proper scoring rules assess the whole probability forecast, while binned diagnostics visualize a particular kind of miscalibration.

Negative log likelihood

For multiclass labels, NLL = −(1/n) Σᵢ log pᵢ,yᵢ. It is a strictly proper scoring rule: in expectation, reporting the true distribution is optimal. It strongly penalizes confident errors, which is useful but can make the average sensitive to a few probabilities near zero.

Brier score and decomposition

For binary predictions, the Brier score is the mean squared probability error:

BS = (1/n) Σᵢ (pᵢ − yᵢ)².

For multiclass predictions, use BS = (1/n) Σᵢ Σₖ (pᵢₖ − 1[yᵢ = k])². Lower is better. With predictions grouped into bins b, the binary score has the approximate Murphy decomposition

BS = uncertainty − resolution + reliability,

where uncertainty = ȳ(1−ȳ), resolution = Σ_b (n_b/n)(ȳ_b−ȳ)², and reliability = Σ_b (n_b/n)(p̄_b−ȳ_b)². Thus the score rewards separating cases with different outcome frequencies and penalizes gaps between forecast probability and observed frequency. “Reliability” here is the calibration-error term, so smaller is better.

ECE and related diagnostics

Partition predictions into bins B₁,…,BM. For top-label calibration, let acc(B_m) be empirical accuracy and conf(B_m) mean confidence in bin m. Expected calibration error is

ECE = Σ_m (|B_m|/n) |acc(B_m) − conf(B_m)|.

  • Adaptive ECE: chooses quantile or otherwise data-dependent bins, commonly with roughly equal counts. It gives sparse confidence regions more influence than fixed-width ECE, but remains bin-dependent.
  • Maximum calibration error: MCE = max_m |acc(B_m) − conf(B_m)|. It surfaces the worst bin but is noisy when bins contain few cases.
  • Reliability diagram: plots empirical accuracy against mean confidence per bin, with the diagonal representing perfect calibration. Include bin counts or a confidence histogram so tiny bins are visible.

Binning sensitivity. Boundaries can merge opposite errors, split a small sample into noisy estimates, or leave tail bins nearly empty. Fixed-width, equal-frequency, and classwise bins can therefore tell different stories. ECE is not a proper scoring rule and a single top-label ECE can hide within-bin, per-class, subgroup, and distribution-shift failures. Report bin choices, uncertainty intervals, sample counts, NLL or Brier score, and important slices rather than treating one ECE value as definitive.

Selective prediction

A selective predictor answers only when its score passes a policy threshold. Let aᵢ ∈ {0,1} indicate that case i is answered and let ℓᵢ be its loss.

  • Coverage: C = (1/n) Σᵢ aᵢ, the fraction answered.
  • Selective risk: R = Σᵢ aᵢℓᵢ / Σᵢ aᵢ, the average loss among answered cases. Define the zero-coverage endpoint separately.
  • Risk–coverage curve: sort cases by confidence and plot selective risk as progressively lower-scoring cases are included. A good ranking keeps risk low at a given coverage.
  • Area under the risk–coverage curve (AURC): summarizes that curve; lower is better for a fixed loss and evaluation distribution. State whether the area is a discrete average or an interpolated integral because conventions differ.

Worked numerical example

Five predictions, ordered by confidence, have scores 0.95, 0.85, 0.75, 0.60, 0.40 and correctness 1, 1, 0, 1, 0. With threshold τ = 0.70, the system answers the first three: coverage is 3/5 = 60% and 0–1 selective risk is 1/3 ≈ 33.3%. The empirical risks at coverages 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, 100% are 0, 0, 1/3, 1/4, 2/5. Using their discrete mean gives AURC ≈ (0 + 0 + .333 + .25 + .4)/5 = .197. Notice that empirical risk need not decrease at every finite-sample step: removing the correct 0.60 case raises risk from 25% at 80% coverage to 33.3% at 60%.

Abstention and cost-sensitive rejection

Choose the abstention threshold on held-out deployment-like data, then freeze it for final testing. If an incorrect answer costs C_error, abstention costs C_abstain, and a calibrated score p is the probability of correctness, answer when (1−p)C_error ≤ C_abstain. For C_error = 10 and C_abstain = 1, this rule answers at p ≥ 0.9. Real policies may also include the cost of a correct answer, delayed escalation, class-specific harms, latency, and capacity constraints. Report both risk–coverage and cost–coverage curves.

Confidence signals for LLMs

No single signal is automatically an answer-level probability. Construct a score on the unit the policy consumes, label that unit with an independently measured outcome, and calibrate the mapping on held-out data.

Do not train the confidence model and report calibration on the same examples.

Epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty

  • Aleatoric uncertainty is irreducible variation or ambiguity under the stated data-generating and observation model: noisy measurements, multiple acceptable labels, or genuinely stochastic outcomes. Better data about the same case does not necessarily remove it.
  • Epistemic uncertainty is uncertainty about the model, parameters, or functions supported by limited or shifted evidence. It may shrink with informative data, a better-specified model, or broader training coverage.

This is an operational, model-relative distinction, not a uniquely observable partition. Misspecification can appear as either type. Predictive entropy alone measures total dispersion of one predictive distribution and does not reveal whether that dispersion comes from ambiguous outcomes or uncertainty over models.

In Bayesian-style classification, a useful identity is H[p(y|x,D)] = E_θ H[p(y|x,θ)] + I(y;θ | x,D). The expected member entropy is often interpreted as within-model ambiguity, while the mutual-information term measures disagreement across parameter samples. Deep ensembles and Bayesian approximations such as variational inference or Monte Carlo dropout provide analogous within-member and between-member signals. These interpretations depend on the posterior approximation, ensemble construction, likelihood, and model specification; they do not recover “true” uncertainty by definition.

To model data noise explicitly, use an outcome distribution appropriate to the task: heteroscedastic regression variance, annotator or label distributions, repeated measurements, or latent ambiguity classes. Then validate the decomposition against interventions such as adding relevant training data, repeating measurements, changing domains, or checking whether disagreement falls where evidence increases. See uncertainty quantification, Bayesian methods and variational inference, and probabilistic foundations.

Claim-level grounding

Answer-level citation presence is too coarse. Evaluate grounding as a claim–evidence relationship:

  1. Segment claims: split the answer into atomic, externally verifiable propositions. Exclude or separately label opinions, instructions, and purely connective text. Human-audit the segmenter because compound claims can hide partial support.
  2. Attach evidence: map each claim to its cited passages and record uncited retrieved evidence separately.
  3. Judge entailment: label each claim–passage pair as entailed, contradicted, or insufficient. An NLI model can assist, but domain experts and exact checks are needed for numbers, dates, negation, tables, and safety-critical claims.
  4. Assess source quality: score authority, freshness, provenance, and independence separately from entailment.

One explicit convention is citation precision = supported cited claim–citation pairs / all evaluated cited pairs, and citation recall = verifiable claims with at least one supporting citation / all verifiable claims. State the counting unit and treatment of redundant citations. Also report claim support rate, contradiction rate, and unsupported material-claim rate.

Calibration methods

Fit calibrators on an independent labeled calibration set drawn from the intended operating distribution. The set must cover relevant classes, confidence ranges, domains, languages, and policy slices; keep a final test set untouched. Calibrating token scores against answer labels, or evidence scores against overall correctness, silently changes the target and is generally invalid.

After model, prompt, retrieval, decoding, label-policy, or population drift, re-check reliability and decision cost. Recalibrate only with sufficiently fresh labeled data and version the calibrator with the entire pipeline. Recalibration can repair a score-to-frequency mapping; it cannot create missing capability, detect every out-of-distribution case, or make a false source true.

Conformal prediction

Conformal prediction converts a fitted score into prediction sets using a held-out calibration set. Define a nonconformity score s(x,y) that is larger when candidate output y fits input x poorly. For split conformal with n calibration examples and target miscoverage α, take the finite-sample quantile at rank ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉. For multiclass classification, one simple score is s(x,y)=1−p_y(x), producing the prediction set Γ(x)={k: 1−p_k(x) ≤ q}.

Under exchangeability of calibration and future examples, the standard construction provides finite-sample marginal coverage of at least 1−α, subject to the usual tie convention. It does not promise conditional coverage for every input or subgroup. Temporal dependence, distribution shift, adaptive reuse of the calibration set, and pipeline changes can violate exchangeability. Report set size along with coverage: a set containing every label is valid but uninformative.

Structured generation makes the output space enormous. Tokenwise conformal coverage does not compose into sequence-level coverage, and conformalizing only sampled candidates cannot cover valid outputs that the generator never proposes without additional assumptions. Semantic equivalence is hard to define, exact-match sets can contain many paraphrases, and sequence-level nonconformity scores may be dominated by length. For selective generation, a set of plausible answers is not the same as an abstention policy: the system still needs a rule for set size, ambiguity, user presentation, and escalation. Grammar or schema constraints can ensure validity, but they do not make exchangeability hold or guarantee factual correctness.

Thesis connection

Gradient regimes may be structured and predictive, but they should not be called epistemic uncertainty without an independent uncertainty target. The HTML thesis note reports interpretable structure in training dynamics, not validated uncertainty estimation. A follow-up could test whether gradient-derived scores predict held-out error, ensemble disagreement, data-scarcity interventions, or selective risk under shift, with calibration performed on separate data. Until then, the careful claim is correlation with structured training regimes rather than an identified epistemic quantity.

Failure modes

  • measuring only citation presence rather than claim support;
  • treating verbal confidence as calibrated probability;
  • using in-distribution calibration on shifted data;
  • collapsing all hallucination types into one score;
  • allowing retrieval to inject unsupported claims;
  • interpreting predictive entropy as isolated epistemic uncertainty;
  • reporting one aggregate ECE without bin, class, or slice diagnostics;
  • calibrating and evaluating on the same examples;
  • mistaking conformal marginal coverage for per-input correctness;
  • optimizing coverage without pricing the harm of errors and abstentions.
