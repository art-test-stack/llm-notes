Diffusion models learn to reverse a gradual noising process. The forward process supplies analytically controlled training pairs at every noise level; a neural network learns the reverse dynamics, which can then be integrated stochastically or deterministically to turn noise into data.

1. Generative-modelling objective

Data distribution

Given samples x₀ ~ p_data(x), generative modelling seeks a normalized model p_θ(x₀) that assigns high probability to the data distribution and can generate new samples. Maximum likelihood minimizes KL(p_data || p_θ), but evaluating and differentiating log p_θ(x₀) is generally difficult when generation uses many hidden steps.

Latent-variable formulation

Introduce latent states x₁:T = (x₁, …, x_T) and define

p_θ(x₀:T) = p(x_T) ∏_{t=1}^T p_θ(x_{t-1} | x_t), p_θ(x₀) = ∫ p_θ(x₀:T) dx₁:T.

Usually p(x_T) = N(0, I). Direct marginalization still appears intractable, so training uses a fixed variational distribution q(x₁:T | x₀).

Why diffusion introduces a tractable corruption process

Diffusion chooses q to be a Markov chain of small Gaussian corruptions. Its transition, any marginal q(x_t | x₀), and the one-step posterior q(x_{t-1} | x_t, x₀) are all Gaussian with closed-form parameters. Consequently, training can draw an arbitrary noise level directly—without simulating all preceding steps—and the variational bound reduces mainly to supervised denoising losses. A long chain also makes each reverse transition locally simple enough to approximate by a Gaussian.

2. Discrete forward process

Choose a schedule 0 < β_t < 1, define α_t = 1 − β_t, and define the cumulative signal retention

ᾱ_t = ∏_{s=1}^t α_s, ᾱ₀ = 1.

The variance-preserving forward transition is

q(x_t | x_{t-1}) = N(√α_t x_{t-1}, β_t I), x_t = √α_t x_{t-1} + √β_t ε_t, ε_t ~ N(0, I).

Here β_t is the newly injected variance, α_t is the signal fraction retained in one step, and ᾱ_t is the fraction of the original signal retained after t steps. The signal-to-noise ratio is SNR_t = ᾱ_t/(1 − ᾱ_t).

Deriving q(x_t | x₀)

Substitute the recurrence into itself:

x_t = √α_t(√α_{t-1} x_{t-2} + √β_{t-1} ε_{t-1}) + √β_t ε_t = √(α_t α_{t-1}) x_{t-2} + √(α_t β_{t-1}) ε_{t-1} + √β_t ε_t = √ᾱ_t x₀ + Σ_{s=1}^t √(β_s ∏_{j=s+1}^t α_j) ε_s.

The sum is Gaussian. Its covariance coefficient telescopes:

Σ_{s=1}^t β_s ∏_{j=s+1}^t α_j = Σ_{s=1}^t (1 − α_s) ∏_{j=s+1}^t α_j = 1 − ∏_{s=1}^t α_s = 1 − ᾱ_t.

Therefore the closed-form marginal and its reparameterized sampler are

q(x_t | x₀) = N(√ᾱ_t x₀, (1 − ᾱ_t)I), x_t = √ᾱ_t x₀ + √(1 − ᾱ_t) ε, ε ~ N(0, I).

This identity is the key computational shortcut: sample t and one ε, then construct x_t in one operation.

3. Reverse process

Learned reverse kernel

The generative chain starts at noise and applies

p_θ(x_{t-1} | x_t) = N(μ_θ(x_t,t), Σ_θ(x_t,t)).

A model may fix Σ_θ = σ_t²I, learn it, or interpolate between schedule-derived bounds. Predicting a diagonal variance is more expressive but needs careful parameterization and often a hybrid likelihood objective.

Exact forward posterior

Conditioning the two relevant Gaussians gives

q(x_{t-1} | x_t,x₀) = N(μ̃_t(x_t,x₀), β̃_t I), β̃_t = β_t (1 − ᾱ_{t-1}) / (1 − ᾱ_t), μ̃_t = [√ᾱ_{t-1} β_t / (1 − ᾱ_t)] x₀ + [√α_t (1 − ᾱ_{t-1}) / (1 − ᾱ_t)] x_t.

After replacing x₀ by its estimate from a noise predictor, the same mean becomes

μ_θ(x_t,t) = (1/√α_t) [x_t − β_t/√(1 − ᾱ_t) · ε_θ(x_t,t)].

Thus the network can learn the mean directly or predict ε, x₀, the score, or v and convert that output to a mean.

ELBO decomposition

Jensen's inequality gives log p_θ(x₀) ≥ E_q[log p_θ(x₀:T) − log q(x₁:T|x₀)] = −L_VLB. Grouping terms yields

L_VLB = KL(q(x_T|x₀) || p(x_T)) + Σ_{t=2}^T E_q KL(q(x_{t-1}|x_t,x₀) || p_θ(x_{t-1}|x_t)) + E_q[-log p_θ(x₀|x₁)].

The first term has no learned parameters for a fixed schedule. The middle terms match learned reverse kernels to tractable posteriors; the last is a reconstruction or decoder likelihood term. With fixed variance and an ε-parameterized mean, each middle KL is a weighted noise-prediction MSE. The common “simple loss” samples t uniformly and drops the ELBO-derived timestep weight; it is easier to optimize but is no longer the literal likelihood bound.

4. Prediction parameterizations

Write a = √ᾱ_t, b = √(1 − ᾱ_t), so that x_t = a x₀ + b ε. These outputs are algebraically convertible only where the formulas' divisors are nonzero: recovering x₀ from ε requires a≠0, recovering ε from x₀ or a score requires b≠0, and either conversion can be ill-conditioned near a schedule endpoint. The v rotation remains algebraically invertible when a²+b²=1, but finite precision and prediction error still matter. Information equivalence is not optimization equivalence: target scale, numerical conditioning, and loss weighting differ.

PredictionTarget / interpretationUseful conversion
εThe standard Gaussian noise added to x₀.x̂₀ = (x_t − b ε̂)/a
x₀The clean sample itself.ε̂ = (x_t − a x̂₀)/b
score s∇_{x_t} log p_t(x_t); during supervised corruption the conditional target is ∇ log q(x_t|x₀).ŝ = −ε̂/b, hence ε̂ = −b ŝ
vA signal/noise rotation: v = a ε − b x₀.x̂₀ = a x_t − b v̂; ε̂ = b x_t + a v̂

The v equations follow because [x_t,v]ᵀ = [[a,b],[-b,a]][x₀,ε]ᵀ, an orthogonal rotation since a²+b²=1.

Weighting consequences

  • For fixed reverse variance σ_t²I, the exact ELBO weight on noise MSE is proportional to β_t²/[2σ_t² α_t(1−ᾱ_t)]. The simple ε loss instead gives each sampled timestep equal explicit weight.
  • Because ||ε̂−ε||² = SNR_t ||x̂₀−x₀||², an unweighted ε loss is an SNR-weighted x₀ loss. Conversely, unweighted x₀ MSE emphasizes low-SNR/noisy times relative to unweighted ε MSE.
  • Because ε = −b s, ||ε̂−ε||² = (1−ᾱ_t)||ŝ−s||². Changing between noise and score prediction without changing the loss changes the noise-level weighting.
  • v targets interpolate between noise at high SNR and negative data at low SNR. Combined with SNR clipping or min-SNR weights, this can balance optimization across noise levels.

Parameterization does not by itself change the ideal reverse dynamics; finite capacity, target conditioning, clipping, loss weighting, and numerical error make the practical models differ.

5. Denoising score matching

The score of the noisy marginal is

s*(x,t) = ∇_x log p_t(x).

The marginal density p_t is unknown, but the corruption kernel has a known conditional score:

∇_{x_t} log q(x_t|x₀) = −(x_t − √ᾱ_t x₀)/(1 − ᾱ_t) = −ε/√(1 − ᾱ_t).

Denoising score matching trains

E_{t,x₀,ε} [λ(t) ||s_θ(x_t,t) − ∇_{x_t}log q(x_t|x₀)||²].

Although the target is conditional on x₀, the MSE-optimal predictor given only x_t is its conditional expectation, which equals ∇ log p_t(x_t) (Fisher's identity). Therefore ε_θ and a score network are related by s_θ(x_t,t) = −ε_θ(x_t,t)/√(1−ᾱ_t). Noise prediction is denoising score matching with a particular noise-level weighting.

6. Continuous-time formulation

Let an Itô SDE gradually transform data at time 0 into a simple prior at time T:

forward SDE: dx = f(x,t) dt + g(t) dW_t.

If s*(x,t)=∇_x log p_t(x), the reverse-time SDE is

reverse-time SDE: dx = [f(x,t) − g(t)² s*(x,t)] dt + g(t) dW̄_t, integrated from t=T down to t=0.

Here dt is negative along the numerical trajectory and W̄ is reverse-time Brownian motion. Intuitively, the original drift remains, while the score term points probability mass back toward high-density regions strongly enough to undo diffusion; Brownian noise preserves the stochastic path law.

Probability-flow ODE

probability-flow ODE: dx = [f(x,t) − ½g(t)² s*(x,t)] dt.

Removing Brownian noise requires halving the score correction. The resulting deterministic ODE has the same one-time marginal densities p_t as the SDE, though not the same trajectories or transition law. It enables deterministic sampling, standard ODE solvers, latent interpolation, and likelihood calculation via the divergence of its vector field.

Variance-preserving and variance-exploding processes

ProcessSDEBehavior
VPdx = −½β(t)x dt + √β(t)dWContinuous limit of DDPM: signal decays while total variance stays bounded for standardized data. Its conditional mean coefficient is exp(−½∫β dt).
VEdx = √(d[σ²(t)]/dt)dWNo drift; progressively adds Gaussian noise so variance grows with σ²(t). The signal is not attenuated, and the terminal scale must dominate the data scale.

The score network, time sampling, loss weights, terminal prior, and solver must all use the same SDE/noise convention. See Stochastic Processes and SDEs for Itô and reverse-time foundations.

DDIM update

For a jump from t to an earlier index s, first compute x̂₀=(x_t−√(1−ᾱ_t)ε̂)/√ᾱ_t, then

σ_{t→s} = η √[(1−ᾱ_s)/(1−ᾱ_t)] √[1−ᾱ_t/ᾱ_s], x_s = √ᾱ_s x̂₀ + √(1−ᾱ_s−σ_{t→s}²) ε̂ + σ_{t→s} z, z ~ N(0,I).

η=0 gives the deterministic DDIM path; nonzero η restores controlled stochasticity.

Number-of-function-evaluation trade-offs

An NFE is normally one network call, the dominant cost. Fewer NFEs reduce latency and energy but increase discretization error. Higher order helps only while the learned vector field is sufficiently smooth; strong classifier-free guidance can increase curvature and make aggressive step reduction unstable. Compare samplers at equal NFE, wall-clock time, resolution, guidance, and randomness—not merely equal step count. Corrector steps, classifier calls, and multistage solvers can require multiple evaluations per nominal step.

8. Conditioning and guidance

Class conditioning

A conditional model receives a label or embedding y: ε_θ(x_t,t,y). Conditioning may enter through concatenation, adaptive normalization, cross-attention, or feature modulation. Direct conditioning learns the conditional distribution but does not provide a test-time strength knob.

Classifier guidance

Bayes' rule gives

∇_x log p_t(x|y) = ∇_x log p_t(x) + ∇_x log p_t(y|x).

A noise-aware classifier supplies the second gradient. Scaling it sharpens class alignment, but requires a separate classifier trained on noisy inputs and can exploit classifier artifacts.

Classifier-free guidance

Train one model on conditional and unconditional examples by replacing the condition with a null token with probability p_drop. At sampling time combine predictions:

ε_CFG = ε_uncond + w(ε_cond − ε_uncond).

Under this convention w=0 is unconditional and w=1 is the ordinary conditional model. Some libraries call s=w−1 the guidance scale and write (1+s)ε_cond−sε_uncond; always check the convention.

Increasing guidance generally improves condition adherence and perceived fidelity while reducing diversity, exaggerating features, and risking saturation or off-manifold samples. Conditional dropout must be large enough to learn a useful unconditional branch but not so large that conditional capacity is starved. Guidance scale, dropout rate, sampler, and NFE interact and should be evaluated together.

9. Latent diffusion

Train an autoencoder with encoder E and decoder D, then diffuse z₀=E(x₀) rather than pixels. A typical image latent is spatially downsampled and has only a few channels, so the denoiser's convolutions or attention operate on far fewer values. Sampling produces ẑ₀, then D(ẑ₀) returns pixels.

  • Compute benefit: lower spatial resolution reduces denoising FLOPs and memory, making larger networks, cross-attention, and higher-resolution generation practical.
  • Reconstruction bottleneck: details discarded by E cannot be recovered by perfect latent denoising; decoder artifacts bound sample quality.
  • Latent geometry: the encoded distribution may not be exactly Gaussian or isotropic, and its scale must match the noise schedule.
  • Limitations: small text, faces, exact geometry, and high-frequency texture can be damaged; a separately trained autoencoder adds data, objective, and deployment complexity.

10. Distillation and acceleration

Progressive distillation

A student learns to reproduce two teacher denoising steps with one step, often halving the step count repeatedly. It retains the teacher's trajectory family but compounds teacher and approximation errors, and usually requires a separate training stage for each reduction.

Consistency models

A consistency function maps any point on the same probability-flow trajectory to a common endpoint, so one or a few evaluations can generate or edit samples. It can be distilled from a diffusion model or trained with consistency constraints directly. Very low NFE is attractive, but optimization stability and one-step quality are demanding.

Few-step diffusion and connection to rectified flow

Few-step systems combine a suitable prediction target, timestep schedule, distillation or adversarial/perceptual objectives, and a solver designed for coarse integration. They trade some coverage or controllability for latency.

Rectified flow and flow matching learn a velocity field that transports a simple prior to data, commonly along straight conditional interpolation paths. Diffusion probability-flow ODEs also define deterministic transport, but their vector field is obtained from a score under a chosen diffusion path. Reflow or rectification can straighten trajectories, reducing curvature and making few Euler-like steps more accurate. The connection is deterministic transport; the training paths and objectives need not be identical.

End-to-end training and sampling pseudocode

This minimal example trains with uniform-timestep ε prediction and samples with DDPM ancestral updates. Arrays alpha_bar include alpha_bar[0] = 1.

# Precompute a discrete VP schedule.
beta = schedule(T)                       # entries 1..T
alpha = 1 - beta
alpha_bar = cumulative_product(alpha)

# Training: one random noise level per data example.
repeat:
    x0 = next_data_batch()
    t = randint(1, T, shape=[batch_size])
    eps = normal_like(x0)
    xt = sqrt(alpha_bar[t]) * x0 + sqrt(1 - alpha_bar[t]) * eps
    eps_hat = model(xt, t, condition=maybe_drop_condition())
    loss = mean(weight[t] * squared_norm(eps_hat - eps))
    optimizer_step(loss)                 # weight[t] = 1 for simple loss

# Sampling: ancestral DDPM, optionally using classifier-free guidance.
x = normal(shape=[batch_size, *data_shape])
for t in range(T, 0, -1):
    eps_u = model(x, t, condition=NULL)
    eps_c = model(x, t, condition=prompt)
    eps_hat = eps_u + guidance * (eps_c - eps_u)

    mean = (x - beta[t] / sqrt(1 - alpha_bar[t]) * eps_hat) \
           / sqrt(alpha[t])
    posterior_var = beta[t] * (1 - alpha_bar[t-1]) \
                    / (1 - alpha_bar[t])
    z = normal_like(x) if t > 1 else 0
    x = mean + sqrt(posterior_var) * z
return x

For unconditional generation, make only one model call. For DDIM or an ODE solver, keep the same trained model but convert its output to the state or derivative expected by that sampler.
