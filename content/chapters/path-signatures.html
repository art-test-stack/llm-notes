A path signature turns an ordered, possibly irregular stream into a hierarchy of geometric features. Level one records displacement, level two records ordered pairwise effects and area, and higher levels record increasingly detailed order interactions.

Core idea. For a path X, its signature is the collection of all iterated integrals of its coordinate differentials. A truncated signature is a finite feature vector; a log-signature is a less redundant coordinate system for the same group-like object; a Neural CDE is a learned dynamical model driven by the path, not another name for either feature vector.

1. Paths and streams

Continuous paths

A path is a map X : [0,T] → ℝd. The parameter is often time, but it may be any variable that orders observations. A path is continuous if nearby parameter values produce nearby points. For ordinary Riemann–Stieltjes integration, bounded variation is a convenient regularity assumption; rough-path theory extends the construction to much more irregular signals.

Piecewise-linear interpolation

A discrete sequence (tn, xn) does not specify a continuous path until an interpolation rule is chosen. Piecewise-linear interpolation joins consecutive observations by straight segments and gives an exact finite computation of a truncated signature. It is causal offline or once a segment endpoint has arrived, but not for continuous strict-online queries inside that segment: with observations (t_{0},x_{0}) and (t_{1},x_{1}), the interpolated value at t_{0}\lt t\lt t_{1} uses the future endpoint x_{1} before it is observed. Previous-value, rectilinear/time-joined, delayed, or explicitly online schemes avoid that look-ahead.

Irregularly sampled time series

Signatures naturally accept irregular observations because they operate on a path rather than a fixed grid. However, if only values are supplied, a reparameterization-invariant signature cannot distinguish slow from fast traversal. Add the actual observation time as a channel when gaps or sampling density carry information.

Multichannel paths

For d channels, X_t=(X_t^1,\ldots,X_t^d). Cross-channel iterated integrals encode ordering: “channel i moved, then channel j moved” differs from the reverse. This is why signatures capture more than per-channel summary statistics.

Time augmentation

Replace Xt by \widetilde X_t=(t,X_t) to retain duration, speed relative to the clock, and irregular sampling gaps. Normalize time only if absolute duration should not matter. Other common augmentations include observation-count, missingness masks, and lead–lag channels.

2. Iterated integrals

Assume first that X has bounded variation. For a multi-index I=(i_1,\ldots,i_k), define

S^I(X)_{0,T}=\int_{0<t_1<\cdots<t_k<T}dX_{t_1}^{i_1}\cdots dX_{t_k}^{i_k}.

The ordered simplex t_{1} \lt \cdots \lt t_{k} is essential: it makes the feature sensitive to the order in which coordinates move.

First-level increments

S^i(X)_{0,T}=\int_0^T dX_t^i=X_T^i-X_0^i.

Geometrically, level one is the displacement vector. It knows the endpoints’ difference but not the route between them.

Second-level signed areas

S^{ij}(X)=\int_{0<s<t<T}dX_s^i\,dX_t^j.

The full second level records ordered pairs of movements. Its symmetric part is fixed by level one through the shuffle identity:

S^{ij}+S^{ji}=S^iS^j.

The antisymmetric part

A^{ij}=\tfrac12\bigl(S^{ij}-S^{ji}\bigr)

is the signed Lévy area in the (i,j) plane, relative to the chord closing the path. Its sign reverses when the order of the two channel movements reverses. Thus level two distinguishes “i then j” from “j then i” even when both paths have the same increment.

Higher levels

Level k contains dk coordinates before algebraic compression. These measure ordered k-way interactions and refine the geometric description. Individual coordinates become harder to interpret, but collectively the hierarchy can characterize a path up to specific equivalences.

Multi-index notation

Writing S(2,1,2) means: integrate a movement in channel 2, followed later by one in channel 1, followed later by one in channel 2. The word or multi-index length |I| is the signature level.

3. Signature definition

Tensor-series representation

Let V=\mathbb{R}^d. The signature is the formal tensor series

\begin{aligned}\operatorname{Sig}(X)_{0,T}&=\bigl(1,S^1(X),S^2(X),\ldots\bigr)\in T((V)),\\S^k(X)&=\int_{0<t_1<\cdots<t_k<T}dX_{t_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes dX_{t_k}\in V^{\otimes k}.\end{aligned}

The scalar level zero makes tensor multiplication and Chen’s identity work cleanly.

Truncated signatures

In computation, retain only levels 0,…,m:

\operatorname{Sig}^{\le m}(X)=\bigl(1,S^1(X),\ldots,S^m(X)\bigr).

Factorial decay

For a bounded-variation path with total variation L, a standard bound is

\lVert S^k(X)\rVert\le\frac{L^k}{k!}.

The factorial denominator arises from integrating over the ordered simplex, whose volume is 1/k! of the full cube. The bound depends on the chosen tensor norm and does not mean high levels are always numerically negligible after arbitrary scaling.

Why truncation is practical

Factorial decay and task-dependent finite resolution often make low levels useful. The raw feature dimension through depth m, excluding level zero, is

D(d,m)=\sum_{k=1}^m d^k=\frac{d^{m+1}-d}{d-1},\qquad d\ne1.
Dimension calculation. With d = 4 channels and depth m = 3, the levels contain 4, 16, and 64 coordinates: 4 + 16 + 64 = 84 features, or 85 if the constant level zero is stored. At depth 5 this becomes 4 + 16 + 64 + 256 + 1024 = 1364, illustrating why depth and channel count must be chosen together.

4. Chen’s identity

Signature of concatenated paths

If X * Y follows path X and then path Y, with the endpoint of X joined to the start of a translated copy of Y, then

\operatorname{Sig}(X*Y)=\operatorname{Sig}(X)\otimes\operatorname{Sig}(Y).

Algebraic product

The product is tensor-series multiplication, not elementwise multiplication. At level k,

\bigl(S(X)\otimes S(Y)\bigr)^k=\sum_{r=0}^k S^r(X)\otimes S^{k-r}(Y).

In particular, S^1(X*Y)=S^1(X)+S^1(Y) and

S^2(X*Y)=S^2(X)+S^1(X)\otimes S^1(Y)+S^2(Y).

The cross term records that every movement in X occurs before every movement in Y.

Worked small example

Let X move one unit along the first coordinate, so its increment is a = (1,0). Let Y then move two units along the second coordinate, with increment b = (0,2). A straight segment with increment v has second level v⊗v/2. Chen’s identity gives

Level 1: a + b = (1, 2) Level 2: ½a⊗a + a⊗b + ½b⊗b = [[1/2, 2], [ 0, 2]]

Therefore S12 = 2 while S21 = 0: the horizontal movement happened before the vertical movement. The antisymmetric area is \tfrac{1}{2}(2-0) = 1, the signed area of the right triangle closed by the chord. Reversing the two segments swaps the off-diagonal entries and changes the area’s sign.

Streaming and windowed computation

Maintain a running truncated signature G. When a new path piece arrives, compute its signature H and update G ← G⊗H. This supports online processing without replaying the full history. Windowed systems can combine signatures of blocks in a tree. Removing an old prefix requires the group inverse, or more simply recomputing from cached blocks; it is not ordinary coordinate-wise subtraction.

5. Invariances and information

Translation invariance

Because signatures integrate dX, adding a constant vector to every point leaves the signature unchanged. If absolute position matters, retain the initial point as a separate feature or add a reference channel.

Reparameterization invariance

For an orientation-preserving, sufficiently regular change of parameter, the path traverses the same geometric trace in the same order and has the same signature. This is valuable when sampling speed is nuisance variation. It is harmful when speed, duration, or dwell time is predictive; time augmentation then restores this information.

Tree-like equivalence

For bounded-variation (finite-length) paths, equal signatures characterize tree-like equivalence. Each tree-like equivalence class has a tree-reduced representative unique up to orientation-preserving reparameterization. Translation invariance is a separate fact because the signature integrates increments dX; retain the initial point separately when absolute location matters. These statements do not reconstruct a unique timestamped raw series and require different machinery for rougher path classes.

Why time or lead–lag augmentation may be needed

Time augmentation separates paths with identical geometric order but different timing. Lead–lag augmentation duplicates channels into lagged and leading copies, turning discrete ordering and quadratic variation into geometric area. It is especially useful when the raw piecewise-linear path would erase discrete effects such as volatility or when repeated points create ambiguity.

What information signatures can lose

  • Absolute starting location, unless stored separately.
  • Traversal speed and calendar gaps, unless time is included.
  • Tree-like backtracking excursions.
  • Fine detail above the chosen truncation depth.
  • Sampling and missingness patterns, unless represented as channels.

6. Log-signatures

Redundancy in raw signatures

Signature coordinates obey shuffle identities; for example, S^iS^j=S^{ij}+S^{ji}. Raw tensor coordinates are therefore not algebraically independent.

Free Lie algebra intuition

The tensor logarithm log Sig(X) lies in the free Lie algebra generated by the path channels. Informally, it keeps primitive directions and their nested noncommutative interactions: channels ei, brackets [ei,ej], then nested brackets such as [ei,[ej,ek]]. The bracket captures order effects that vanish in a commutative summary.

Dimensionality reduction

A basis such as a Lyndon or Hall basis stores only independent Lie coordinates. By Witt’s formula, the number at homogeneous level k is

\ell k(d) = (1/k) \sum r\mid k \mu (r)d k/r.

For d=4, levels 1–3 contain 4, 6, and 20 log-signature coordinates, for 30 total rather than 84 raw signature coordinates.

Computational trade-offs

Log-signatures reduce feature dimension and remove shuffle redundancy, but computing the tensor logarithm and converting to a Lie basis add cost and implementation complexity. The log-signature of a concatenation is not simple addition when increments do not commute; it is combined through the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff series. Raw signatures are often simpler for Chen updates and kernels.

7. Rough paths

Why highly irregular paths require enhanced information

For smooth or bounded-variation controls, integrals against dX are classical. For signals as irregular as Brownian motion, first-level increments alone do not determine limits of nonlinear differential equations driven by smooth approximations. One must also specify consistent higher-order iterated integrals—most notably area. A rough path is this enhanced path.

p-variation

The p-variation of X measures accumulated roughness:

\lVert X\rVert_{p\text{-var};[0,T]}=\left(\sup_{\Pi}\sum_{[u,v]\in\Pi}\lVert X_v-X_u\rVert^p\right)^{1/p}.

Smaller admissible p means greater regularity. A finite p-variation driver is enhanced through at least level ⌊p⌋ in the basic rough-path construction. Brownian sample paths have finite p-variation for every p\gt 2, so level-two area is essential.

Controlled differential equations

A controlled differential equation (CDE) has the form

dz_t=f(z_t)\,dX_t,\qquad z_0\ \text{given}.

The solution responds to the ordered geometry of the control path. Rough-path theory makes the solution map continuous in a suitable rough-path metric: close enhanced drivers yield close solutions. This stability is the pathwise foundation behind learning from irregular controls.

Signature as a universal feature set at an appropriate level

On suitable compact sets of reduced paths, linear functionals of signatures can approximate continuous path functionals. The shuffle algebra supplies products of coordinates, enabling a Stone–Weierstrass-type universality argument. This does not say a shallow truncation solves every task: the enhancement level required to define the driver and the truncation depth required for prediction are separate choices.

8. Machine-learning use

Linear models on signatures

Compute \operatorname{Sig}^{\le m}(X) or \log\operatorname{Sig}^{\le m}(X), then fit linear or generalized-linear models. The feature map contains nonlinear path interactions while the predictor remains convex, data-efficient, and inspectable. Regularization is important because feature counts grow rapidly.

Neural CDEs

A Neural CDE parameterizes the vector field with a neural network:

dz_t=f_\theta(z_t)\,dX_t,\qquad \widehat y=g_\theta(z_T).

It consumes a continuously interpolated control and learns a nonlinear state update. Unlike a precomputed signature, its representation is task-trained and solver-dependent. The relationship is structural: CDE solutions can be expanded in iterated integrals, but a Neural CDE is a model, not a log-signature.

Signature kernels

A signature kernel evaluates an inner product between potentially very high or infinite signature feature maps without explicitly materializing every coordinate. Kernel methods can preserve the signature geometry, though kernel-matrix memory, numerical approximation, and large dataset scaling remain concerns.

Hybrid neural-signature models

Options include feeding windowed log-signatures to an MLP or Transformer, using signatures to summarize local blocks before attention, learning a channel embedding before signature computation, or using signature layers inside a differentiable model. These hybrids trade explicit geometric inductive bias against end-to-end flexibility.

Online and irregular time-series applications

Chen updates make signatures natural for streaming classification, event data, finance, sensor monitoring, and medical records. Time and missingness channels preserve irregular observation structure. Windowed or decayed summaries are often preferable when the data distribution changes over time.

9. Practical design

Interpolation choice
Use piecewise linear as a transparent baseline. Use rectilinear, causal, spline, or domain-specific interpolation only when its between-sample assumptions match the process. Avoid letting future observations affect past interpolated values in forecasting.
Missing values
Distinguish “unobserved” from “observed at zero.” Add masks and time-since-last-observation channels; fit imputation parameters on training data only. A path library generally expects finite values, so unresolved NaNs need an explicit policy.
Scaling
Standardize or nondimensionalize channels before taking signatures. A channel scaled by c scales a level-k word containing it r times by cr. Fit scalers within each training fold.
Truncation depth
Start at depth 2 or 3, then tune using validation data. Deeper is not automatically better: it raises dimension, variance, and numerical range.
Channel dimension
Every extra channel multiplies high-level growth. Remove redundant channels, project to a justified lower dimension, or use grouped/local signatures when the raw dimension is large.
Windowing
Choose windows according to the prediction horizon and causal information set. Overlapping windows increase sample dependence; split by entity and time before constructing them when leakage is possible.
Computational growth
A raw depth-m signature has \sum d k nonconstant coordinates. Runtime and memory also depend on path length, batching, algorithm, and whether log-signatures or kernels are used. Measure end-to-end cost rather than relying only on feature-count formulas.

10. Evaluation and failure modes

Leakage from interpolation

A noncausal spline or bidirectional imputation can allow a feature at prediction time to depend on future observations. Construct the full preprocessing pipeline separately inside each training fold and audit which timestamps contribute to every prediction.

Excessive feature dimension

Large d and m cause exponential raw-feature growth, multicollinearity, and overfitting. Compare raw signatures with log-signatures, regularize, lower the depth, reduce channels, and report memory and latency alongside accuracy.

Time-order ambiguity

A poor embedding of discrete events can make distinct sequences trace the same or tree-like-equivalent path. Time, lead–lag, event-count, or visibility transforms can disambiguate order—but each changes the inductive bias and must be validated.

Lack of invariance when needed

Augmentations deliberately break invariances. Absolute time may make a model sensitive to harmless clock changes; retaining starting value may hurt shift robustness. Decide from the task which transformations should leave predictions unchanged, then test those transformations explicitly.

Benchmarking against simpler sequence models

Compare against domain baselines, summary statistics, lag features, linear autoregression, trees, RNNs, temporal convolutions, and attention or state-space models as appropriate. Match splits, parameter tuning, input information, and compute budgets. A signature method earns its complexity only if it improves predictive quality, sample efficiency, robustness, interpretability, or streaming cost.

Evaluation checklist. Use entity- and time-aware splits; fit interpolation, imputation, scaling, channel selection, and truncation on training data; test timing and translation perturbations; ablate each augmentation; measure feature count and latency; and include a simple sequence baseline.
