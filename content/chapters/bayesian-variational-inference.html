Learning objectives

You should be able to derive the evidence lower bound (ELBO), explain pathwise gradients and mini-batch scaling, diagnose the limitations of a mean-field approximation, compare common neural-network uncertainty methods, and turn parameter uncertainty into posterior predictive uncertainty.

Notation

Let D = {(x_i, y_i)}_{i=1}^N be the observed data, w the model parameters, p(w) the prior, and p(D | w) the likelihood. The variational family is q_\phi (w). For supervised iid data, log p(D | w) = Σ_i log p(y_i | x_i, w).

1. Bayesian learning

Bayesian learning treats unknown parameters as random variables. Before observing data, beliefs are encoded by a prior p(w). A model of how parameters generate observations gives the likelihood p(D | w). Bayes' rule combines them:

p(w | D) = p(D | w) p(w) / p(D) p(D) = ∫ p(D | w) p(w) dw

The posterior p(w | D) represents updated parameter uncertainty. Prediction averages over that uncertainty rather than selecting one parameter vector:

p(y* | x*, D) = ∫ p(y* | x*, w) p(w | D) dw

This posterior predictive can widen where data are sparse and contract where data constrain the model.

Difference from MAP training

Maximum a posteriori (MAP) training returns one mode:

w_MAP = argmax_w [log p(D | w) + log p(w)]

For an isotropic prior w ~ N(0,\sigma ^{2}I), negative-log-posterior MAP minimizes -Σ_i log p(y_i|x_i,w) + ||w||²/(2σ²), up to a constant. If the data loss is averaged over n observations, the matching coefficient becomes 1/(2n\sigma ^{2}). This is L2 regularization added to the objective. Multiplicative or decoupled optimizer weight decay, as in AdamW, is not generally equivalent under adaptive optimization; the familiar equivalence holds for ordinary SGD with consistent learning-rate and scaling conventions. MAP still discards posterior width, correlations, and alternative modes. Full Bayesian prediction integrates over parameters; MAP usually predicts with p(y* | x*, w_MAP).

2. Intractable posterior inference

The denominator in Bayes' rule is the marginal likelihood, or evidence:

p(D) = ∫ p(D | w) p(w) dw

It averages the likelihood over every possible parameter setting. This integral both normalizes the posterior and rewards models that explain the data across a substantial region of prior mass rather than at only one finely tuned point.

For a neural network with millions or billions of weights, the integral is high-dimensional and the likelihood is a nonlinear, non-conjugate function of w. Exact summation on a grid grows exponentially with dimension; symbolic conjugate integration does not apply; and even evaluating all important modes is impossible because symmetries and nonlinearities produce a complicated posterior geometry. Exact posterior prediction contains another integral over this unavailable posterior. Consequently, neural-network Bayesian inference relies on approximations such as variational inference, local Gaussian approximations, or sampling methods whose costs are themselves substantial.

3. Variational inference

Variational inference (VI) chooses a tractable family q_\phi (w) and fits its parameters \phi so that q_\phi (w) approximates p(w | D). The conventional objective minimizes the reverse KL divergence:

φ* = argmin_φ KL(q_φ(w) || p(w | D))

ELBO derivation

Insert q_\phi (w) / q_\phi (w) into the evidence and apply Jensen's inequality:

log p(D) = log ∫ p(D, w) dw = log ∫ q_φ(w) [p(D, w) / q_φ(w)] dw = log E_q [p(D, w) / q_φ(w)] ≥ E_q [log p(D, w) - log q_φ(w)] = E_q [log p(D | w)] - KL(q_φ(w) || p(w)) ≡ ELBO(φ)

The same result follows from an exact decomposition:

log p(D) = ELBO(φ) + KL(q_φ(w) || p(w | D))

Because log p(D) is constant with respect to \phi and KL is non-negative, maximizing the ELBO is equivalent to minimizing KL(q_\phi \mathbin{\Vert} p(w \mid D)).

The expected log-likelihood term E_q[log p(D | w)] is often called the data-fit or reconstruction term: sampled weights should explain the observations. The KL-to-prior term constrains the approximate posterior to remain near p(w). Maximizing the ELBO therefore balances fit against complexity:

maximize: expected log likelihood − divergence from prior minimize: negative expected log likelihood + KL-to-prior

“Reconstruction” is most literal for latent-variable generative models. In supervised prediction, “expected log-likelihood” is the clearer name.

4. Mean-field Gaussian approximation

A common choice assigns an independent Gaussian to every weight:

q_φ(w) = ∏_j Normal(w_j; μ_j, σ_j²) φ = {μ_j, ρ_j}_j σ_j = softplus(ρ_j) > 0

This doubles the number of learned scalars relative to a point estimate but makes sampling and the KL to a Gaussian prior cheap. For p(w) = \mathcal{N}(0, \tau ^{2}I), the KL is analytic:

KL(q || p) = ½ Σ_j [(σ_j² + μ_j²)/τ² − 1 + log(τ²/σ_j²)]

The factorization cannot represent posterior correlations, curved valleys, heavy tails, or multiple separated modes. Neural-network weights are strongly coupled: permuting hidden units can describe the same function, while rescaling adjacent layers can create extended posterior ridges. Independent marginals can therefore give misleading uncertainty even if their individual variances look plausible.

Mode-seeking behavior of KL(q \mathbin{\Vert} p)

Reverse KL heavily penalizes placing q where p has little density, but it does not penalize missing regions where p has mass if q assigns them none. With a restricted unimodal family and a multimodal target, the optimum often covers one mode instead of bridging modes. This “mode-seeking” behavior commonly underestimates uncertainty. It is a tendency induced by the KL direction and family restriction, not a guarantee that every VI posterior is too narrow.

5. Reparameterization trick

Directly differentiating through a random draw w ~ q_\phi is awkward because the sampling operation depends on \phi. For a diagonal Gaussian, move the randomness into a parameter-free variable:

ε ~ Normal(0, I) w = μ + σ ⊙ ε

Now an expectation can be written E_\varepsilon [f(\mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon )], and automatic differentiation follows the deterministic path from \mu , \sigma through w to the loss. These pathwise gradients use how the same random draw changes smoothly with \phi, and usually have much lower variance than score-function estimators such as f(w) \nabla _\phi \operatorname{log} q_\phi (w).

Mini-batch scaling and Monte Carlo estimates

For a uniformly sampled mini-batch B of size M, an unbiased estimator of the full-data ELBO is:

ELBO_B ≈ (N/M) Σ_{i∈B} log p(y_i | x_i, w) − KL(q_φ(w) || p(w)) where w = μ + σ ⊙ ε, ε ~ Normal(0, I)

The factor N/M scales only the mini-batch likelihood sum; the global KL appears once per full-data objective. If code averages likelihood over the batch, multiply that mean by N. An alternative divides the KL across the number of mini-batches, but the two conventions must not be mixed.

With S weight samples, replace the expectation by (1/S) \sum _s \operatorname{log} p(D \mid w^{s}). One sample per mini-batch is often sufficient during training because successive batches provide more Monte Carlo draws. More samples reduce estimator noise at higher compute cost; prediction commonly uses many more samples. Local reparameterization and Flipout-style estimators can further reduce gradient variance by decorrelating perturbations across examples.

6. Stochastic variational inference

Stochastic variational inference (SVI) combines the ELBO with data subsampling and stochastic optimization. The scaled mini-batch likelihood gives an unbiased estimate of the data sum, enabling Robbins–Monro updates or optimizers such as Adam without scanning the entire dataset for every step.

In classical conjugate exponential-family models, natural gradients precondition updates by the inverse Fisher information of the variational family. They follow the geometry of distributions rather than Euclidean parameter coordinates and can yield especially simple, stable updates for global variational parameters. In non-conjugate neural VI they are possible but less common because Fisher-vector computations or approximations add complexity; ordinary adaptive gradients are the usual baseline.

Local versus global latent variables

  • Global variables, such as mixture proportions or neural-network weights, are shared by all observations. Their posterior uses evidence from the whole dataset and their variational parameters persist across mini-batches.
  • Local variables, such as a topic assignment or per-example latent code z_i, belong to one observation or group. A mini-batch updates only its local factors, often by coordinate ascent, optimization, or an amortized inference network.

SVI typically estimates local factors for the sampled data, then uses their sufficient statistics to update the global factors with the appropriate population scaling. Amortization avoids storing a separate variational parameter vector for every example, but introduces an amortization gap if the inference network cannot reach each example's best local approximation.

8. Prior design

  • Gaussian priors: w_j ~ Normal(0, τ²) are computationally convenient and make mean-field KL terms analytic. The scale \tau controls shrinkage and must be interpreted relative to architecture, normalization, fan-in, and parameterization.
  • Hierarchical priors: learn or infer shared scales, for example w_j ~ Normal(0, τ²) with \tau ~ \text{half}-\text{Cauchy}. They let groups of weights adapt their regularization strength and can encode layer- or unit-level structure.
  • Scale mixtures: a mixture of narrow and wide Gaussians can place substantial mass near zero while retaining heavy tails for large weights. The KL may then require Monte Carlo estimation.
  • Function-space consequences: priors over weights induce priors over functions, and seemingly harmless iid weight priors can imply implausible output scales, smoothness, or correlations. Architecture and prior cannot be assessed independently.
  • Prior sensitivity: weak data, out-of-distribution inputs, and non-identifiable neural parameters expose prior choices most strongly. Sensitivity analysis should vary defensible prior families and scales, then compare posterior predictions rather than only weight histograms.

Hierarchical and heavy-tailed priors are more expressive, but an inflexible variational family may fail to inherit their useful dependencies or tails. A sophisticated prior does not repair a severely misspecified q_\phi.

9. Posterior predictive uncertainty

Given approximate posterior samples w^{s} ~ q_\phi (w), estimate the posterior predictive with:

p(y* | x*, D) ≈ (1/S) Σ_s p(y* | x*, w^(s))

Use independent or appropriately decorrelated samples and report Monte Carlo error when S is small.

Regression decomposition

If each model predicts y | x,w ~ Normal(m_w(x), v_w(x)), the law of total variance gives:

E[y* | x*, D] ≈ mean_s m_s Var[y* | x*, D] ≈ mean_s v_s + variance_s m_s aleatoric uncertainty epistemic uncertainty

The first term is expected observation noise; the second measures disagreement in conditional means across weight samples. This decomposition is model-dependent: misspecification can move unexplained error between the two terms.

Small Bayesian regression example

Consider y_i = w x_i + η_i, with known \eta _i ~ \mathcal{N}(0, 0.2^{2}) and prior w ~ Normal(0, 1). For x = [-1, 0, 1] and y = [-2.1, 0.1, 1.9], conjugacy gives:

posterior variance = (1/1² + Σ_i x_i²/0.2²)⁻¹ = 1/51 ≈ 0.0196 posterior mean = posterior variance × Σ_i x_i y_i/0.2² = (1/51) × 100 ≈ 1.9608 w | D ~ Normal(1.9608, 0.0196)

At x^{\ast} = 1.5, the predictive mean is 1.5 \times 1.9608 \approx 2.94. The predictive variance is:

0.2² + 1.5² × 0.0196 ≈ 0.0841 noise parameter uncertainty predictive standard deviation ≈ 0.29

A VI implementation would learn an approximate Gaussian for w, sample w^{s} = \mu + \sigma \varepsilon ^{s}, and recover the predictive mean and variance by Monte Carlo. This one-dimensional conjugate result is a useful correctness check for the ELBO, reparameterization, and uncertainty decomposition.

Classification uncertainty and calibration

For classification, average probability vectors, not class labels or logits:

p(y*=k | x*, D) ≈ (1/S) Σ_s softmax_k(f_{w^(s)}(x*))

Predictive entropy summarizes total uncertainty. The mutual information between predictions and sampled weights—predictive entropy minus expected per-sample entropy—is a common epistemic-disagreement measure, while the expected entropy reflects ambiguity within each sampled model. Neither is automatically calibrated.

Check probabilistic predictions with negative log-likelihood, Brier score, reliability diagrams, and expected calibration error, including important subgroups and shifted data. Calibration means that events assigned probability p occur about a fraction p of the time; it does not guarantee accuracy, sharpness, or useful out-of-distribution detection. Post-hoc temperature scaling can improve calibration on validation-like data without fixing the posterior approximation.

10. Scaling limitations

  • Parameter count: storing \mu and \sigma for every weight approximately doubles model parameters; optimizer states multiply this further.
  • Memory and compute: posterior sampling adds random-number generation and often requires multiple forward passes. Training must retain gradients for variational parameters and may need distributed synchronization for both sets.
  • Approximation misspecification: a low ELBO optimization error does not imply a good posterior if the variational family cannot express its shape. Predictive quality can hide poor uncertainty away from the training distribution.
  • Correlated weights: mean field ignores strong within-layer and cross-layer dependencies. Full covariance is quadratic in parameter count; low-rank, Kronecker-factored, block-diagonal, and matrix-variate approximations trade fidelity for tractability.
  • Cold-posterior phenomena: neural networks sometimes predict better when the likelihood is tempered as p(D \mid w)^{1/T} with T \lt 1, producing a posterior sharper than the nominal Bayesian posterior. Possible causes include likelihood misspecification, data augmentation, prior mismatch, duplicated or curated data, and approximate inference. The phenomenon is a diagnostic, not a universal prescription to tune temperature on the test set.
  • Large language models: a variational distribution over every pretrained parameter is usually prohibitive. Practical approximations place uncertainty only over adapters, LoRA factors, a last layer, a prediction head, selected blocks, or a low-dimensional subspace; combine a frozen backbone with ensembles; or fit local Laplace/SWAG-style approximations. These are parameter-efficient posterior approximations, not full Bayesian inference over the entire LLM.

Scaling decisions should start from the uncertainty needed by the downstream decision. If a small ensemble, calibrated head, or last-layer posterior meets that need, a full mean-field distribution over a foundation model is unlikely to justify its cost.

Method selection summary

  • Start with MAP when only point accuracy and a cheap baseline are needed.
  • Use MC dropout when retrofitting repeated stochastic predictions into an existing dropout model matters more than posterior fidelity.
  • Use Laplace for a post-hoc local posterior around a good MAP solution, especially when a last-layer or structured curvature approximation is sufficient.
  • Use mean-field VI when an explicit learned weight distribution and online variational objective are important, accepting its independence assumptions.
  • Use deep ensembles as the strong practical uncertainty baseline when multiple trainings and model copies are affordable.
  • Use SWAG when SGD checkpoints are available and a cheap correlated local Gaussian is attractive.

Failure modes

  • scaling the KL by N/M together with the likelihood, thereby over-regularizing;
  • sampling once at evaluation and calling the result a posterior predictive;
  • averaging classification logits instead of predictive probabilities;
  • equating a narrow mean-field posterior with certainty rather than approximation bias;
  • using weight-space variance as a direct proxy for function-space uncertainty;
  • comparing methods without matching architecture, data, augmentation, and compute;
  • reporting calibration only in-distribution or only as one aggregate ECE number;
  • calling ensembles Bayesian without specifying a posterior or member-weighting argument.
